# How Today's Fastest Growing B2B Businesses Found Their First Ten Customers

**Source:** Lenny Rachitsky (Lenny's Newsletter), ~2020  
**URL:** https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-todays-fastest-growing-b2b-businesses  
**Status:** read_full

---

## Core Argument

Only three strategies exist for finding first B2B customers (versus seven for consumer): tap your personal network, seek customers where they are, and get press. The overwhelming pattern is that nearly every successful B2B company used *both* personal network and community/channel targeting — the real question is not which, but how far your network takes you before you need to pivot to community outreach. Press rarely drives first customers and is almost never the primary mechanism.

The research covers: Slack, Stripe, Salesforce, Shopify, Figma, Segment, Square, Gusto, Plaid, Asana, Airtable, New Relic, Atlassian, Okta, Intercom, Workday, Coda, Looker, Carta, Amplitude.

---

## Strategy 1: Tap Your Personal Network

The dominant early mechanism. Sources of network: friends and former colleagues, investors, incubator peers (YC connections especially), alumni networks, people encountered via prior products.

**Company examples:**

- **Slack:** "We begged and cajoled our friends at other companies to try it out" (Stewart Butterfield). Started with 6-10 companies through personal network before any broader push.
- **Stripe:** Used YC network; founders would set up accounts directly on prospects' laptops at the moment of consent. First user was a personal acquaintance.
- **Salesforce:** Marc Benioff leveraged prior investment contacts. A PM met a prospect in a grocery store line (discovered pain with ACT! and Excel). Network density = serendipity density.
- **Gusto:** First 10 customers were YC batch peers and family/friend connections starting CA businesses.
- **Asana:** "Almost all our first ~15 users are people we're personally close to" (Justin Rosenstein). Focused on making them happy before expanding.
- **Workday:** "Earliest customers were CEOs who knew the founders. They bought the friendship, not the software" (Dave Duffield). For high-trust/high-ACV products, the first purchase IS a personal trust transaction.
- **Okta:** CEO set a goal of 15-18 net new conversations monthly using LinkedIn alumni networks and angel investor connections.
- **Intercom:** "All day every day I'd email people by hand... 100% by hand" (Des Traynor). Leveraged prior business relationships from previous company.
- **Coda:** First alpha user was a YouTube colleague. When they churned after one day, the negative feedback proved valuable for product improvement.
- **Looker:** Acquired first 10+ customers through personal, customer, and investor networks. Success formula: make early customers extremely happy and implement evangelist programs.
- **Carta:** First customers from angel investors and sister portfolio founders through investor connections.

---

## Strategy 2: Seek Customers Where They Are

Identify the specific communities where target customers already congregate and engage directly — in their language, on their platform.

**Community targeting examples:**

- **Shopify:** Ruby on Rails developer community; beta waitlist from RoR community had thousands; several 2006 cohort members still selling today.
- **Figma:** Dylan Field identified influential designers on Twitter by filtering for people who personally inspired him, then cold-emailed and sought introductions to show them the product. Taste-driven curation of early adopters.
- **Segment:** Launched as open-source library on Hacker News, "blew up overnight." Early customers were small company founders on the platform.
- **Plaid:** Became their own first customer (frustration with existing solutions). Grew through developer/PM community word-of-mouth: forums, IRC, meetups, accelerator networks.
- **Square:** Jack Dorsey's personal network gave early users but not repeat customers. Real traction came from walking into local merchant locations (Lilybelle Flowers, Sightglass Coffee).
- **Atlassian:** Open source communities, mailing lists, newsgroups, IRC, and affordable self-serve pricing to lower entry barriers.
- **New Relic:** Targeted Ruby on Rails developers specifically; earned endorsements from community thought leaders DHH and Tobi Luetke — credibility via quality, not paid placement.
- **Amplitude:** First customers were YC cohort peers who had the same data retention questions Amplitude was solving. Problem-first community match.

---

## Strategy 3: Get Press

Rarely the primary driver for first customers. Works best when there's a genuinely novel story to tell.

- **Twilio:** Launched via TechCrunch with a clever PR angle — generated awareness and credibility in developer community.
- **Slack:** Press blitz that generated 15,000 signups in two weeks — but note that Slack already had word-of-mouth momentum; press amplified it, didn't create it.

---

## The Collison Installation

One of the most tactically memorable examples: when a Stripe prospect agreed to try the beta, Patrick or John Collison would say "Give me your laptop" and set them up right there. Zero friction at the critical moment of consent. The insight: the gap between "yes I'll try it" and "I've actually started using it" is where most early trials die. Close that gap with immediate, hands-on activation.

---

## The YC Network Effect

YC connections appear in Stripe, Gusto, Amplitude, Segment, and others. The incubator is not just capital — it is a pre-built trust network of technically sophisticated early adopters who (a) understand the product, (b) will give honest feedback, (c) have genuine pain points aligned with the founders', and (d) have a cultural norm of helping other founders. This is the strongest single structural advantage in the first-customer problem.

---

## Key Structural Insights

1. Only three B2B strategies (vs. seven for consumer) — the search space is narrow. Don't try to invent a fourth.
2. Both personal network AND community targeting are used by almost all successful companies. They are sequential, not alternatives.
3. The real sequencing question: "how far does my network take me, and when do I pivot to community?"
4. Personal trust is load-bearing for high-ACV products (Workday, Okta). For low/mid-ACV developer tools, community credibility substitutes.
5. Being embedded in the relevant community (Ruby/Rails for Shopify and New Relic, Figma designers on Twitter, developer forums for Plaid) is structurally superior to cold outreach to that same community.
6. Evangelist programs compound: Looker's formula — make early customers extremely happy → build evangelist programs → the network effect of happy customers brings the next wave.

## VERSO RELEVANCE

### §12 — GTM: Cold Start and First Customers

**[CONFIRMS]** Verso's primary channel (X build-in-public + Show HN) maps to Strategy 2 (seek customers where they are). The target community is already identified: structurally-aware senior/AI engineers who are on GitHub, Hacker News, and Twitter/X. This is the same community where Segment, Plaid, New Relic, and Atlassian found their first customers. The confirmation is empirical across a wide range of developer tools. **Verdict: CONFIRMS the channel selection. Lock in.**

**[CONFIRMS]** The Figma example — Dylan Field curating early adopters by personal taste (influencers who inspired him personally) — is the exact Verso superconsumer-first strategy. The first 50 users should be hand-selected AI engineers whose work is already admired by the Verso founder, not found via cold outreach or paid channels. Curation > volume in the first cohort. **Verdict: CONFIRMS superconsumer-first approach with additional texture on the curation mechanism.**

**[NEW-IDEA]** The Collison Installation is the most tactically actionable pattern: when a senior engineer says "I'm curious about this," the Verso response should be "let me pull up your repo right now." The barrier between consent and activation must be zero. Concretely: the founder should be able to pip install verso, wrap an existing agent in <10 minutes in a live call, and show the eval report in the same session. This is the Verso equivalent of "give me your laptop." **Verdict: adopt-now — define the Verso Collison Installation procedure for early conversations.**

**[SHARPENS]** The New Relic example sharpens the community credibility mechanism: endorsements from DHH and Tobi Luetke were not purchased or asked for — they emerged from genuine product quality. Verso's equivalent is a blog post or Twitter thread from a recognizable AI engineer (Anthropic alumni, notable OSS contributor) organically sharing how they use it. Target this as a leading outcome, not a PR campaign. **Verdict: sharpen §12 — community credibility comes from quality, not placement. Define the target endorsers.**

**[NEW-IDEA]** The Workday framing — "they bought the friendship, not the software" — has a specific implication for Verso's early pricing. If the first customers are personal network contacts, they are buying access and trust, not a fully validated product. This means: (a) free or nominal pricing for the first cohort is correct (removes the commercial friction while preserving the relationship), (b) the explicit quid pro quo is feedback and co-design, not revenue. Do not try to charge personal network contacts at a price that reflects future product quality — charge for current reality or give free access. **Verdict: adopt-now — first cohort = free access + feedback agreement, not commercial pricing.**

### §1 — Moat / Bet

**[SHARPENS]** The YC network advantage is relevant for Verso: if the founder has YC-adjacent relationships (former batch members, YC-funded companies building agents), these should be the first 5 targets. They have (a) technical sophistication, (b) genuine agent pain, (c) cultural norm of helping. The structural advantage here is not access (everyone can email YC companies) but *trust* — which comes from prior relationship or shared community context. **Verdict: sharpen — map personal network to YC-adjacent AI engineering teams as first targets.**

### Founder-OS Layer

**[NEW-IDEA]** The evangelist program as a compounding mechanism (Looker formula: make first customers extremely happy → build evangelist programs) is a founder-OS principle: the return on making the first 10 customers delighted is not $10k ARR, it is the network effect of word-of-mouth in a tight-knit community. In the AI engineer community, one public endorsement from a respected engineer is worth 10 cold outreach campaigns. **Verdict: adopt-now — define Verso's evangelist activation: what does "make them extremely happy" look like, and what's the follow-on ask?**

**[CONFIRMS]** The personal network sequencing principle (exhaust your network first, then pivot to community) is the right operating order for Verso. Start with direct outreach to senior AI engineers the founder already knows or has interacted with. Only when that is exhausted (or signals weak) pivot to community channels. This prevents premature reliance on channels that require a cold trust-building phase. **Verdict: CONFIRMS — encode in founder-OS as the sequencing rule for first-customer acquisition.**
