## Core Thesis

When AI commoditizes execution — generating UI, writing code, producing designs — the last non-replicable moat is taste: the organizational capacity to make things that are good, coherent, and beautiful in ways that feel inevitable. AI does not eliminate design; via Jevons Paradox, it *increases* demand for good design by making bad design faster and cheaper to produce.

---

## Key Arguments

### 1. The Taste Moat (Central Claim)

- "When AI commoditizes execution, taste is the last thing that doesn't get commoditized."
- Taste is not subjective — it has real, learnable properties: "taste is not subjective — it has real properties you can learn to see, shown by the fact that designers measurably improve over time."
- Dylan's method for systematically developing taste: deliberate study across domains (visual arts, comics, narrative design, recommended reading: *Understanding Comics*, *Codex Seraphinianus*), pattern recognition in what makes things beautiful vs. functional, and building organizational muscle around quality judgment.
- The *Brat Summer* album cover example: the iconic chartreuse-and-blurry-text aesthetic was not generated by AI. It required aesthetic conviction — someone had to believe it was right before the market confirmed it. AI cannot supply conviction; it can only optimize for prior approval signals.
- "Good enough is going to be mediocre. You're going to need to differentiate through design, craft, point of view, brand, storytelling, and marketing."

### 2. Removing Blockers > Adding Features (Product Prioritization Rule)

- "Removing blockers beats adding features as a product-prioritization rule."
- "The highest-leverage product prioritization move is removing blockers, not adding features."
- Figma's early strategy divided work into two streams: **blockers** (features preventing adoption/use) and **differentiators** (design systems, component sharing). Addressing the blockers unlocked adoption; the differentiators built retention.
- When asked to name Figma's best product work, Dylan points to UX friction reduction, not new capability launches.

### 3. Time to Value (TTv Obsession)

- Dylan's north-star metric for every product Figma ships: reduce the time from first interaction to meaningful result.
- Applies to every new product line (FigJam, Slides, Sites, Draw, Make) and every feature addition.
- In a crowded market with AI-generated alternatives, fast time-to-value determines adoption and retention before a user can even evaluate competing tools.

### 4. Jevons Paradox Applied to Design

- AI handles routine design tasks → frees designers for strategic work.
- Paradox: AI *increases* demand. Faster creation leads to more design requests, not fewer. "AI is a capability multiplier, not a replacement. It increases demand for design via the Jevons Paradox."
- "AI makes it so designers are able to get rid of drudgery...instead, they'll be able to think more holistically, think further, and go beyond."
- Figma's 2025 AI Report: designers increasingly view AI as a collaborative partner rather than a threat — framing shifts from "replacement" to "capability multiplier."
- Implication: as AI makes mediocre design cheaper, the signal-to-noise ratio falls — *good* design becomes more valuable, not less, because it stands out more.

### 5. Product Line Philosophy

- Figma evaluates adjacent products (Slides, FigJam, Sites, Draw, Make) against: does this fit an existing workflow designers already do? Does it maintain the quality bar? Does it create ecosystem lock-in without sacrificing individual product excellence?
- "Figma Make" = AI-powered prototyping (timestamp 45:14) — generating functional prototypes from design files.

### 6. Post-Adobe Acquisition: Equanimity as Operating Principle

- After the $20B acquisition attempt failed (EU/UK regulatory block), Dylan adopted "equanimity" — maintaining consistent execution regardless of deal outcome.
- Focus: independent vision, IPO as validation and capital source, accelerating product innovation.
- Lesson: company coherence requires the CEO to hold the product vision through external volatility without the product strategy bending to deal timelines.

### 7. AI's Effect on Designer Value (not displacement)

- AI eliminates repetitive tasks (spec-writing, resizing, asset generation) but cannot supply: aesthetic judgment, cultural relevance, conviction before market validation.
- "AI makes it so designers are able to get rid of drudgery...instead, they'll be able to think more holistically."
- Dylan rejects the displacement framing explicitly — the question is which parts of design AI absorbs vs. which parts remain human.

## VERSO RELEVANCE

### [SHARPENS §5.5] "Removing blockers first" as the eval and reliability priority rule

Dylan's framework — blockers before differentiators — is a direct product-prioritization input for Verso's roadmap. In §5.5, embedded evals are framed as a quality-assurance layer. Dylan's rule sharpens this: the *first* eval criterion should be "does the agent reliably complete its task without the user needing to intervene?" (blocker removal), before evaluating whether the agent's output is brilliant (differentiator). Reliability eval precedes quality eval.
One-line verdict: sharpens eval priority — measure "did it fail the user" before measuring "was it optimal."

### [CONFIRMS §12] Taste as the framework moat (anti-overkill positioning)

Dylan's "taste is the last moat" directly confirms Verso's positioning hook: the anti-framework stance is a taste claim. The decision to keep the loop <200 lines, reject forced graphs for tactical agents, and ship simple defaults is an organizational taste assertion — that overkill is as wrong as under-building. The "no overkill / no bullshit / simplicity" invariant IS a taste position. The framework with taste wins; the framework without it is a commodity.
One-line verdict: confirms §12 — simplicity as a taste claim is a moat, not just a preference.

### [NEW-IDEA] Jevons Paradox applies to agent infrastructure too

Dylan's Jevons framing for designers applies to Verso: as AI agents become cheaper and easier to build (better base models, more accessible tooling), the *number of agents* organizations want to run will increase, not decrease. The demand for agent infrastructure — context engineering, eval harness, durable checkpointing — grows as agents proliferate. Verso should plan for a world where organizations run 10-100x more agents than today, and the harness that manages them (not the individual agent loop) is the bottleneck. This is a capacity and positioning argument for investing in §6.2 multi-agent orchestration and §6.1 durable layer early.
One-line verdict: new capacity argument for §6.1/6.2 — Jevons says agent infra demand compounds as agent creation gets easier.

### [SHARPENS §4] "Time to value" as the tactical seam metric

Dylan's TTv obsession maps directly to the tactical-to-durable seam (§4). For tactical agents, the relevant "time to value" is: how quickly does a developer get a working agent from the Verso loop? For durable agents, it's: how quickly does the one-flag promotion (§4) work without requiring a rewrite? The seam's value proposition IS a TTv claim — developers don't rewrite, they promote. TTv for the seam = time from "tactical agent working" to "durable agent running with checkpoint/resume."
One-line verdict: sharpens §4's seam — define and measure TTv for the tactical-to-durable promotion path as the seam's north-star metric.
