## Faithful Digest

### The Core Thesis

Category design is a discipline co-equal with product design and company design. The Category King (or Queen) of any given market captures, on average, **76% of the total economics of that market** — leaving all competitors fighting over 24%. Category kings also carry market capitalizations **5-8X higher** than comparable high-growth companies.

The argument: most businesses compete for existing demand within categories others defined. Category design is about creating new demand by redefining the category itself. The question is not "how do we win this game?" but "how do we change the game entirely?"

**Quote (corroborated via search):** "Category Kings/Queens capture two-thirds of their category's economics." (Note: the 76% / two-thirds figure appears in different formulations across Category Pirates content; both are cited.)

### The "Better" Trap

The single most actionable concept for product positioning. Comparison language — "-er, -est, 'more than,' 'faster than,' 'cheaper than'" — unconsciously locks a company into competing within an existing category. If you say "we're like X but better," you've already conceded that X defines the category and that you're playing their game.

Examples of the trap:
- "We're like Slack, but better for async teams"
- "We're like LangChain, but simpler"
- "We're like LlamaIndex, but faster"

The alternative: design a new category with its own name and problem framing, so the comparison never arises.

**Quote (paraphrased from guide):** "Businesses that compete for demand fall into product comparison conversations, often in categories with existing leaders. They walk into these existing markets saying, 'We do the thing the already established Category Queen does, just better/faster/smarter/stronger/cheaper.'"

### The Magic Triangle

Three elements that must be synchronized — all three, equally, or the strategy fails:

1. **Product Design**: Create offerings with "product/category fit" rather than product-market fit. The product must *embody* the new category, not just compete in an old one.

2. **Company Design**: The organizational structure, business model, and culture must be aligned with the category being created. A category-creating product launched by a conventionally-structured company will fail because the company can't support it.

3. **Category Design**: Developing new market context — the problem framing, the language, the customer education — that pulls customers in and establishes category leadership.

A breakthrough product + innovative business model + new category framing = highest probability of becoming a Category King.

### Point of View (POV)

The POV is the foundation of category creation. It is not a positioning statement. It is a declaration of what problem the world has that customers don't yet know how to name.

Classic examples:
- **Keurig**: "single-serve coffee" — not better coffee makers, a new category of coffee experience
- **iRobot**: "robot vacuum cleaner" — not a better vacuum, a new category of home automation

Both examples involve genuine category creation, not feature competition. The POV declares an *enemy* (the old way) and a *promised land* (the new way).

### Languaging

Strategic word choice that educates customers on new distinctions. The words you invent or adopt become the walls of the category you're creating.

Examples:
- "Streaming video" (vs. "cable")
- "Electric guitar" (vs. "acoustic guitar")
- "Cloud computing" (vs. "hosted servers")

Each term signaled a fundamentally different experience, not a marginal improvement.

The practical test: if your company's language could equally describe a competitor, you haven't created new languaging.

### Superconsumers

Superconsumers are approximately **10% of a market** but drive **30-70% of sales** and **100% of category insights**. They are:
- Emotionally invested in the category
- Price-insensitive
- Evangelists who spread the category definition
- The only users worth listening to for category design signals

Key statistic: **Superconsumers spend 3-7X more** than average consumers.

Strategic implication: design for superconsumers first, then let them evangelize to the mass market. Do not design for the median user at the expense of the superconsumer.

### DAMing Demand

Rather than competing for existing demand (the marketing default), "dam" the demand by interrupting the customer's existing purchasing stream and educating them that they actually need something different.

The mechanism: shift the customer's thought from "I want X" to "I actually need Y." This requires category education, not product promotion. The company is in the education business.

### Three Marketing Wars

1. **Information Wars**: Frame the problem, own the narrative, control how the category is understood before competitors define it for you.

2. **Air Wars**: Strategic demand creation at scale — thought leadership, media, analyst relations.

3. **Ground Wars**: Tactical conversion and lead generation once the category framing is established.

Most companies skip directly to Ground Wars. Category designers win by establishing Information Wars dominance first.

### The 8 Common Objections (Publicly Available Framing)

The article addresses eight reasons people claim category design "doesn't work":
1. Can't create demand from nothing
2. Too expensive
3. Product/marketing/price matter more
4. People resist change
5. Better to follow macro trends
6. 24% of large markets is still lucrative
7. Difficult to explain to stakeholders
8. Product positioning is safer

The counterargument to all eight: if you use Netflix, Amazon Prime, Uber, or Chipotle, you already believe in category design — you've been educated into new categories without realizing it.

### The Category Design Scorecard (5 Areas, 0-2 scale)

1. Clear category point-of-view addressing fundamental (not incremental) problems
2. Vision of a problem-free future for the customer
3. Radically different offer and business model
4. Data flywheel for anticipating demand
5. Customer transformation outcomes (not just feature outcomes)

### IPO Implications

**60% of IPOs fail because** conventional wisdom encourages companies to describe themselves in comparison to existing categories ("we're the X of Y") — sameness language that fails to communicate differentiation. Category design gives companies a framework for closing "The Languaging Gap" between what they intend to communicate and what investors/markets perceive.

### Niching Down as Category Creation

Seven vectors for creating categories as small or startup businesses:
- **WHAT**: unique expertise
- **WHO**: specific audience
- **WHEN**: urgency/importance intersection
- **WHERE**: premium positioning
- **WHY**: missionary purpose
- **OUTCOME**: transformational value
- **COST**: premium pricing as a signal of category leadership (not a barrier)

### Economic Proof Points

- Tesla: $0 ad spend → $700B+ market cap (vs. legacy auto spending billions on ads)
- Pepsi: ~$5B on advertising with modest market share gains — competing for demand in a category Coke already defined
- Meta: $100B+ metaverse investment — capital alone cannot force category creation
- Sushirrito (sushi + burrito): small business demonstrating niche category creation at any scale

## VERSO RELEVANCE

### [SHARPENS §12 positioning/GTM — HIGH VALUE] The "Better Trap" is Verso's most dangerous enemy
Every AI agent framework positions itself as "easier," "more powerful," "more flexible" than LangChain / LlamaIndex / CrewAI. If Verso says "simpler than LangChain," it has stepped into the Better Trap and conceded that LangChain defines the category. The Category Pirates framework demands Verso create a new category name and problem framing that doesn't invite direct comparison.

Candidate category framing: Verso is not "a better agent framework" — it is "the durable agent runtime." The problem is not "LangChain is too complex" but "no framework treats agent reliability and context engineering as first-class citizens." This reframes the category and makes LangChain irrelevant (it's not competing for the same crown).  
**Verdict: adopt-now** — this is the most urgent GTM implication from this source

### [SHARPENS §12 positioning/GTM] 76% category king economics justify the category-creation bet
The Category Pirates statistic — category kings capture 76% of economics vs. 24% for all others — quantifies the stakes. Verso doesn't need to win a feature comparison; it needs to define and own the "durable agent runtime" category. Second place in a commodity framework market is economically marginal.  
**Verdict: sharpen**

### [NEW-IDEA §12 positioning/GTM] Superconsumers as Verso's design target
The Superconsumer framework (10% of market, 30-70% of sales, 100% of insights, 3-7X spend) maps directly to Verso's ideal early adopter: ML engineers and senior backend engineers who are *obsessed* with agent reliability, who have hit the walls of LangChain, who care deeply about evals. These are Verso's superconsumers. Designing for them first — not for the median developer who wants a copilot wrapper — is the right strategy.  
**Verdict: adopt-now** — use to focus design decisions on the 10%

### [SHARPENS §1 the bet] Information Wars: own "durable agent runtime" before anyone else does
The category pirates sequence — Information Wars → Air Wars → Ground Wars — suggests Verso's sequencing: first, establish the problem framing ("why durable agents matter," "what the tactical/durable seam is") through essays, blog posts, and open conversation, *before* launch. Win the language war first. Verso's technical blog + open-source release should be primarily category-education content, not feature documentation.  
**Verdict: sharpen**

The Category Pirates POV framework requires identifying an enemy (old way) and promised land (new way). Verso has this: Old way = "choose between quick-and-fragile tactical agents or complex orchestration graphs that require rewriting."
