## Core Argument

Ankit Gupta's central claim: getting first users is **a search problem, not a persuasion problem**. You are not trying to convince skeptics — you are finding the small slice of the population who already wants your product.

> "It's unlikely that a billboard reaches them vs a targeted cold email or a knock on their door."

---

## Key Framework 1: The Minimum Evolvable Product (MEP)

Gupta distinguishes MEP from MVP:

| Concept | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| **MVP** | "Does this solve the problem at all?" |
| **MEP** | "Can this adapt to what we learn?" |
| **MLP** | "Will users love this enough to tell others?" |
| **MMP** | "Is this good enough to compete in the market?" |

The MEP must "survive contact with a tiny group of people and adapt." The key word is *evolvable* — not just viable. The metaphor: products evolve from "amoebas" (basic functionality exposed to market pressures) toward complex mature organisms through an "evolutionary search through the tree of potential future directions."

**Critical implication:** The early adopters don't just give feedback — they *steer* the product's evolutionary direction. The Tesla Roadster example: performance-focused sports-car enthusiasts shaped Tesla's DNA all the way through mass-market models.

---

## Key Framework 2: Two Types of Early Adopters

1. **Natural early adopters:** people who intrinsically enjoy trying new products
2. **People with burning problems:** those who will try anything because the pain is acute

Early adopters with burning problems are "rarely price sensitive" — charge them real money early. Paying customers give sharper, more committed feedback.

---

## Key Framework 3: The 100 Users Test

Before scaling:
> "If you can't get 100 passionate users, you have a product problem, not a distribution problem."

This is a diagnostic threshold. Inability to find 100 fans is a signal to stop and fix the product, not to seek better channels.

---

## Key Framework 4: The Five-Phase Search Algorithm

1. **Prepare** — Build MEP, identify where early adopters live, prepare outreach
2. **Search** — Launch early, use direct outreach, move past skeptics quickly
3. **Learn** — Study users deeply, charge real money, run experiments
4. **Evolve** — Let early users shape product direction
5. **Expand** — Broaden acquisition search only after product-market fit

---

## Acquisition Channels Hierarchy

For early-stage:
- Personal network (first)
- Cold outreach (targeted emails, LinkedIn, direct DMs — not mass blast)
- One community (Reddit, Twitter, industry Slack/Discord, in-person events)

Never attempt all channels at once. Pick one community and go deep.

---

## Cold Email Framework (from secondary YC guides, aligned with this talk)
- 6-8 sentences maximum
- Plain text (no HTML)
- Conversational language, no jargon
- Social proof element
- Clear call-to-action (specific meeting request)
- Volume math matters: to get 2 customers at 20% close rate → need 10 demos → need 20 replies at 5% reply rate → need 400 emails

**The volume insight:** most founders fail because they send 20 emails, get 1 reply, and conclude "outreach doesn't work." The volume causes false conclusions.

---

## Monetization Stance

Never give free access. Use money-back guarantees (30-60 days) rather than free trials. Charge from day one to:
- validate genuine value
- attract customers with committed feedback
- avoid the false-positive of users who "use it" but don't need it

---

## Path Dependency Warning

The product you build with your first 100 users will encode their preferences into the architecture and roadmap. Choose early adopters deliberately — who you start with determines what you become.
