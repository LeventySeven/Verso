## Full Digest

### Central Thesis
McKenzie's argument is deceptively simple: every week of professional effort should produce a *portable, compounding asset* — not just an hour-for-pay swap that vanishes when you leave. The core mantra: **"Don't end the week with nothing. Prefer to work on things you can show. Prefer to work where people can see you. Prefer to work on things you can own."**

The origin of the principle: McKenzie worked 70-90 hour weeks at a Japanese megacorporation. When asked how he stayed motivated for the "91st through 95th hours," he answers: "because I wanted to end the week with something." The framing device is his observation that his day job turned him into a processor — "emails and tickets as input and delivered (occasionally) working code and Excel files as output" — and his situation never improved. **"I'd end the week with nothing."**

### Applied Capitalism: The Capital Formation Frame

McKenzie's philosophical anchor is that employees should transition from "selling hours of labor" to "renting access to accumulated capital as soon as humanly possible." He agrees with a Marxist friend on this point, which he notes is a strange alignment.

He identifies **three non-monetary capital types** that a day job structurally inhibits:
1. **Human capital** — skills developed over time and the value they produce
2. **Social capital** — the ability to call on trusted contacts for assistance or recommendations  
3. **Reputational capital** — how your expertise is perceived in professional circles

Jack Welch's framing for why day jobs don't build this: **"You work for a week, collect your paycheck on Friday, and then you and the company are even."**

The company benefits from your capital formation while you're employed; you don't take it with you.

### Section 1: Prefer Working On Things You Can Show

Developers embrace OSS because it provides "portable capital between companies" — it transfers when you leave. When work is public, you can *show* it; showing it is evidence, not just a claim.

The limitation of traditional references: "credible evidence that a mid-level engineering manager at a company you've never heard of in Nagoya thought I was a really effective employee" provides minimal signal to someone who doesn't know that manager.

**Specific mechanics he recommends:**
- Choose job roles where your work can be publicly demonstrated
- Choose language stacks commonly OSS'd (Rails) over proprietary (C#)
- Choose high-visibility projects (Android) over hidden infrastructure (AdWords plumbing)
- Choose open industries over closed ones

Beyond OSS: creative credits, academic citation counts, and building **artifacts** — conference presentations, blog posts, OSS projects around the generalizable lessons from your work.

On starting before you're good: McKenzie wrote 2006-2008 content "where I was struggling through not understanding anything I was doing" with "less readers than my younger brother's blog on writing superhero novels." It was still valuable years later because he could point to experiments from 2008, then follow-ups in 2009-2010 showing they were "really successful." **The longitudinal trail is the asset.**

Critical directive: **"If you cannot build things you can show at work, you should build things you can show outside of work."**

On IP assignments: companies are "gradually becoming more reasonable." Even his "very straight-laced Japanese megacorp" made exceptions for OSS work outside company hours and his Bingo Card Creator project. His pitch: **"If you let me continue working on these, I'm going to learn lots of skills which I can put to the use of the company."** If they won't: **"OK, in lieu of the side projects, I'll need more money."**

### Section 2: Prefer To Work Where People Can See You

The reframe is crucial — not "work in public" (which suggests rock star status), but developing **"substantial profile among the audience of 'people professionally relevant to them'"** — potentially as small as "a couple of dozen folks" with hiring authority in your area.

**The "poach list" concept:** McKenzie maintains informal mental catalogs of "The people who, if I had infinite money and they had no other commitments, I'd hire to work on a particular project." He has lists organized by expertise (Rails, A/B testing, email writing). His advice: optimize to be on those lists.

**Developer evangelism as a case study in visibility compounding:** Evangelists consistently transition to better jobs afterward. The reason: **"evangelists literally spent years meeting thousands of people and showing them 'Hey, I'm going to live code in front of you while also making my employers fat stacks of money.'"** This contrasts sharply with infrastructure roles — "The Build Guy" who **"suffers in underappreciated solitude."**

Directive: **"If you cannot gain exposure at your day job, try to get some exposure outside of it."** Methods:
- Attend local technical meetups AND business-side industry events (not just one)
- Speak at conferences
- Solicit feedback on your creations
- Build an audience through blogging and email lists

Key insight on minimum viable audience: **"for landing a new job, having an audience of one hiring manager is a darn sight better than having no audience at all."**

**Amy Hoy's "stacking the bricks" metaphor** (quoted by McKenzie in full):

> "Seen from the outside, you might say 'That person with an impressive career? It's like they have a sheer wall made out of awesome. I could not hope to ever have a wall like that.' Seen from the inside, it looks like one day of delivering a single good conference talk, a few weeks spent writing an OSS library, another day writing the definitive blog post on getting multiple Ruby versions playing together, a few months shipping a product used by many people, an hour recording a podcast."

**"Brick by brick, stone by stone, the wall gets higher."**

### Section 3: Prefer To Work On Things You Can Keep

Standard employment: "You give us an hour and, in return, we give you some consideration for that hour." Employees don't bank hours or benefit from them long-term.

He defends Silicon Valley employee equity as "one of the single best innovations in the history of capitalism" — non-managerial employees owning "plus or minus 20% of Twitter, Facebook, etc." versus "plus or minus 'rounding error'" elsewhere. But he warns against overconcentration: don't buy employer stock in company DRIPs or IRAs. You already have too much exposure.

The critique of startup equity narrative as sold to employees: professional investors understand "90% of all shares of early-stage startups are worthless," yet suggest employees have "99% of your net worth tied up in the illiquid shares of one particular company."

His alternative: standard financial advice (broad index funds, Vanguard target retirement) PLUS side projects via sweat equity.

Personal example: his first "$2,000 from Bingo Card Creator turned into Chipotle stock at an average price of $50 a share." He emphasizes this was fortune, not advice-worthy.

**Critical warning about OSS and GitHub:** He loves open source but cautions against immediately publishing on GitHub as a primary distribution channel. GitHub helps developers consume code but doesn't expose work to non-technical stakeholders or decision-makers. **"If you're going to spend weeks or months of time writing meaningful OSS libraries, make a stand-alone web presence for them."**

His A/Bingo example: "probably the best option for Rails A/B testing, by dint of being the only serious option." He secured consulting work through being "The A/B Testing Guy." Creating documentation, quickstart guides, logos, and branded presence **"beats the heck out of having a junior engineer at a potential client just git clone my Github URL and never have my work exposed to a decisionmaker there at all."**

He adds, wryly: **"I don't work for Github, I work for me."**

For side project mechanics: Jason Cohen's Microconf 2013 formula: **"Predictable acquisition of recurring revenue with an annual pre-pay option with a product which solves a demonstrable, enduring pain point for a business."**

### Closing: Consumption vs. Creation

**"Rolling up your sleeves and actually shipping something is much, much more valuable. If you take no other advice from me ever, ship something."**

On failure: "You'll learn more shipping a failure than you'll learn from reading about a thousand successes."

Counterintuitive encouragement: **"people greatly overestimate how difficult this is."**

Final mantra: **"Just don't end the week with nothing."**
