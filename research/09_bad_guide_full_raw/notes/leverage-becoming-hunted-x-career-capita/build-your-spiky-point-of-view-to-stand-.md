## What the Source Actually Argues

### Definition

A "spiky point of view" is a perspective others can genuinely disagree with. Kao defines it: "a belief you feel strongly about and are willing to advocate for. It's your thesis about topics in your realm of expertise." The core test: if everyone agrees with it, it's too middle-of-the-road.

### What Makes a POV Spiky (not just an opinion)

Kao is strict about the distinction between a spiky POV and a hot take / generic contrarianism:

1. **Debatable but defensible** — it invites disagreement yet is grounded in evidence. Not a proven universal truth, but not a random opinion either.
2. **Educational** — it should help the audience "see their problem in a new way." Pure controversy with no insight fails the test.
3. **Rooted in expertise and experience** — "your spiky points of view should be about topics where you are credible and that are relevant for your audience. Otherwise it's a random hot take or opinion, and opinions are cheap."
4. **Authentic** — it emerges from "your unique combination of experience, skills, personality, instincts, and intuition." This makes it "almost impossible to imitate."
5. **Advocacy-shaped** — you must be "brave enough to advocate for what you believe in," not passively float it.

### Spiky vs. Contrarian: The Critical Distinction

"A spiky point isn't controversial for the sake of it. There's nothing more irritating than a contrarian who just wants to stir the pot."

The distinction is: contrarianism is performed; a spiky POV is earned through real engagement with a domain. The energy and conviction level of authentic belief is what lands — you cannot fake it.

### Real Examples from Wes Kao's Own Work

She demonstrates with six specific claims from her expertise:

- "Successful launches require sustaining momentum after the initial event, not just executing a one-time occurrence."
- "Focus on learning to receive feedback rather than give it — most people struggle with reception."
- "Prototyping is unnecessary in 90% of cases. Scenario planning (30 minutes) beats five-day sprints for identifying logical gaps."
- "'Start with why' has done more harm than good" — it enables navel-gazing when audiences only care about solutions.
- "Marketing experiments carry hidden setup and maintenance expenses; deploy them only when results will demonstrably change future behavior."
- "Online teaching is 50% instruction, 50% entertainment."

### Development Method

Kao offers a five-question self-discovery process:
- Which 3–5 beliefs define your craft?
- What invites genuine disagreement?
- What frustrates or angers you professionally?
- When have audiences experienced "revelation moments"?
- Which accepted practices contradict your experience?

She notes that initial answers are often too generic — the first-pass needs to be sharpened. Example refinement: "teach as you learn" → "If you don't have it all figured out—this is a feature, not a bug," with specific frameworks for expert vs. beginner paths.

### The ELU Criterion

"You'll know you have a great spiky point of view when your audience's eyes light up." She calls this "ELU" (Eyes Lighting Up). Market feedback — replies, comments, visible recognition — confirms resonance regardless of whether the audience agrees.

### Why It's a Competitive Moat

A genuine spiky POV is "almost impossible to imitate." When you parrot someone else's stance, your energy level and conviction won't be the same. Authenticity becomes contagious and detectable. This makes a spiky POV one of the few truly non-replicable positioning assets.

## VERSO RELEVANCE

### [SHARPENS] §12 Positioning — The specificity-of-conviction test for VERSO's own POVs

Wes Kao's essay is directly applicable to VERSO's open positioning questions. VERSO already has the intellectual raw material ("harness-is-moat," "we don't force the graph," the seam is structural not cosmetic) but the framework forces a harder question: are these genuinely spiky — i.e., defensible, inviting real disagreement from real experts — or are they just "different from the mainstream" in an un-anchored way?

The specificity test: "prototyping is unnecessary in 90% of cases" is spiky because it's a precise, falsifiable claim from experience. "The harness is the product" is closer to spiky but still needs a concrete, falsifiable edge: something like "every team that rewrites their first LangChain prototype ships a harness anyway — we just make that the starting point."

**Verdict:** Use Kao's five development questions to sharpen each VERSO positioning claim. Generic rebellion is not spiky; domain-grounded precision is.

For VERSO, this maps directly: the 3-line side-by-side demo / one-flag durable diff is the positioning claim made visceral. The prior established principle is "ban '-er-than-LangChain' positioning." Kao adds the structural reason: "-er-than" framing is contrarianism (performing disagreement), whereas VERSO's actual spiky claim is a domain-grounded thesis about where the production failure mode lives (the harness, not the loop). This reframes the non-goal from "don't say we're better" to "our positioning is rooted in a specific failure mode observation, not in comparison."

**Verdict:** VERSO's spiky POV is: "Every production agent team converges on building the same harness — we codified it." That is a falsifiable, expertise-backed claim. "We're better than LangChain" is not spiky, just competitive.
