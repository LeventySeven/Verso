## Core Thesis

Most companies are *positioned by default* — they never made an explicit positioning choice, so their product occupies whatever mental slot customers assigned it first. Default positioning is almost always wrong because it was set before the company understood who actually buys, for what reason, and against what alternative. Dunford's whole practice is built on the claim that positioning is a deliberate, revisable choice, not a one-time declaration.

*"Positioning defines how your product is a leader at delivering something that a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about."*

Positioning is not messaging, taglines, brand stories, vision statements, or mission statements. Those all come *after* positioning. Positioning is the foundation that makes all of them coherent.

---

## The Frame-of-Reference Function

Positioning works like the opening scene of a film. Before a single plot point is explained, the audience absorbs: where we are, what era, what genre, who to root for. Dunford uses *Apocalypse Now* — viewers grasp Vietnam War, protagonist trauma, and tonal register within minutes, before any exposition.

Applied to products: when a buyer hears "CRM," they immediately assume a Salesforce-class competitor, a certain feature set, a certain price band, and a certain target user. Category choice *sets assumptions* before you say anything else. Poor category choice forces your entire marketing and sales motion to fight against the buyer's automatic assumptions instead of leveraging them.

*"Our expectations for products in each of those categories differ significantly based on how they're positioned."* (email vs. chat example)

## Signs of Bad Positioning

- Sales cycles drag; close rates are low
- Customers constantly ask "who are you like?" or compare you to the wrong thing
- Each function (marketing, sales, CS, product) describes the product differently — the founding story has drifted
- You lose 40% of B2B deals to "no decision" (the status quo), which means the buyer never understood why the switch was worth it

---

## The Five Components of Effective Positioning

These five components must be resolved *in order*, because each constrains the next.

**1. Competitive Alternatives**
What would your best customers actually use if you didn't exist? Not just direct competitors — often spreadsheets, an intern, a manual process, or doing nothing. B2B: 40% of lost deals go to "no decision," meaning the alternative is the status quo. You must position against the thing that is actually winning the deals you lose.

**2. Unique Attributes**
The features and capabilities you have that your competitive alternatives lack. Must be grounded in real comparisons against the actual alternatives identified in step 1, not imagined competitors.

**3. Value (and Proof)**
The customer outcome your unique attributes make possible. Features enable benefits; benefits deliver value in the context of the customer's actual goals. Dunford is strict here: value claims require proof — "irrefutable data collected through our own solution's performance, and preferably backing from third parties."

**4. Target Market Characteristics**
The segment of customers who get so much value from steps 1–3 that they buy quickly, avoid discounts, become champions, and refer others. *"Target as narrowly as you can to meet your near-term sales objectives. You can broaden the targets later."* B2B segmentation is firmographic + behavioral (e.g., "three-person creative agencies using Notion with $20K budgets"), not just personas.

**5. Market Category (Frame of Reference)**
The context you choose for the buyer. Three styles:
- **Head-to-Head**: win an existing category against the incumbent leader (requires being genuinely best)
- **Big Fish, Small Pond**: carve a subsegment with different requirements from the market leader (most common for non-leader startups)
- **Create a New Game**: define a new category where you're automatically the leader (highest risk, highest reward — *paleo snack* vs. *diet muffin*)

**+1. Relevant Trends**
Layer on trends that make the problem urgent for enterprise buyers who need to justify procurement to a budget committee. But: *"It's always better to be a little boring than completely baffling."*

## The Traditional Positioning Statement Is Broken

The classic Mad Libs template ("For [segment] who [need], [Product] is a [category] that [benefit], unlike [alternative]...") is *"not only pointless but potentially dangerous."* It assumes one correct answer. Reality: most products can occupy multiple valid positions. The right position depends on what competitive alternative you're displacing, which segment you're targeting, and what market moment you're in.

## The 10-Step Process (abridged)

1. Identify best customers (bought fast, never discounted, refer others, love it)
2. Form cross-functional positioning team: senior rep from marketing, sales, product, CS
3. Release baggage — agree to treat current positioning as a hypothesis, not a fact
4. List true competitive alternatives your best customers actually use
5. Isolate unique attributes vs. those alternatives
6. Map attributes to value themes from the customer's perspective
7. Determine which segment cares most about those themes
8. Choose market frame of reference (head-to-head / subsegment / new category)
9. Layer in relevant trends if they add urgency without adding confusion
10. Document and share across the organization

Timeline: ~one week of focused workshops, depending on internal alignment severity.

## Real-World Examples

- **Help Scout:** Differentiation was treating customers as humans, not ticket numbers. Value: exceptional service driving customer loyalty. Best-fit segment: DTC/e-commerce brands where loyalty = repeat revenue.
- **Postman:** "A platform for building and using APIs" was technically correct but opaque. Positioning work clarified what market they actually occupied.
- **Magic Leap:** Overpromised as a consumer product (vague "revolution"), then successfully repositioned into B2B manufacturing.
- **Segway:** "Revolution in human transport" let buyers imagine flying cars. When reality arrived, disappointment was structural.
