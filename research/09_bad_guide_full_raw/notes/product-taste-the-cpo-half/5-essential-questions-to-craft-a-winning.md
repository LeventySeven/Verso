## What Roger Martin Actually Argues

**Thesis:** Strategy is an integrated set of choices that compels desired customer action. Most companies don't play to win — they play to participate, which means they have no real strategy. The five-question cascade forces integration and real choices.

### The Strategy Choice Cascade (5 questions)
1. **Winning Aspiration** — What are you trying to accomplish? Sets the context for all other choices.
2. **Where to Play** — On what playing field? Which customer segments, geographies, channels, product categories? You cannot play everywhere.
3. **How to Win** — On that chosen playing field, how will you create more customer value than competitors, or be lower-cost?
4. **Must-Have Capabilities** — What capabilities do you need that your competitors don't have, to win where you've chosen to play?
5. **Enabling Management Systems** — What systems must you build and maintain to support those capabilities?

The integration requirement: these five answers must fit together and reinforce each other. Strategy is not any one of these questions alone — it's the set that coheres. "It's not saying what do you want for lunch; it's saying what kind of diet will keep you healthy."

### Playing to Win vs. Playing Not to Lose
Playing not to lose = making choices designed to avoid being wrong, rather than to win. Results in defensive roadmaps, feature parity, and risk avoidance at every level.

The test: if a competitor lowers price and you can't match it profitably, they have the low-cost position — not you. If customers flip coins between you and a competitor, you're not differentiated.

> "You'll know you're playing to play if customers can flip a coin between you and a competitor."

Most strategy is playing not to lose disguised as strategy.

### On Other Strategy Thinkers
- **Hamilton Helmer (7 Powers):** fits inside the "how to win" box — a categorical model of winning modes. Practical, non-academic.
- **RBV (resource-based view):** "I've seen it used once in 42 years of consulting." Academically dominant but practically useless.
- Martin's own view: strategy requires both Porter's positioning AND capability-building — "it's both and."

### Strategy at Every Level
The P&G example: the Head and Shoulders brand manager makes strategic choices that matter. 10% of S&P 500 CEOs are ex-P&G because P&G trains everyone to think strategically, not just the top.

> "People down the organization have to make really important strategic choices, or bad things are going to happen."

### The Key Quote on Skill
> "I have never met this mythical beast called a great natural strategist. Great strategists have one thing in common: they just practice."

## VERSO RELEVANCE

### [CONFIRMS] §12 Positioning — cascade applied to Verso's strategy
Running the cascade on Verso:
1. **Winning aspiration:** be the harness every structurally-aware senior AI engineer reaches for when taking an agent to production (not vibe-coding; not research).
2. **Where to play:** Python, ~5–20 person AI-eng teams, 2nd/3rd production agent, teams that have *already* burned on a toy harness or graph-forced framework.
3. **How to win:** the only harness that makes durability a one-flag seam (not a rewrite), with in-process evals built in — winning on structural integrity, not feature count.
4. **Must-have capabilities:** the seam abstraction (no graph forced), eval corpus aggregator, inspect() legibility, HITL-ready without a new architecture.
5. **Enabling systems:** dogfood-first feedback loop; eval corpus as flywheel; DX-led organic GTM via superconsumers.

This confirms the harness-is-moat bet is coherent as a cascade, not just a tagline. The cascade makes it testable: does the "how to win" flow from the "where to play"? Yes: targeting structurally-aware engineers who need the 2nd/3rd production agent specifically means they care about durability-seam and evals, not about low-friction onboarding.
**Verdict:** run this cascade explicitly in any positioning or strategy document — it stress-tests coherence.

### [SHARPENS] §12 Positioning — Helmer 7 Powers lives inside "how to win"
Martin explicitly places Helmer as a categorical model of *how to win*, subordinate to the full cascade. This is useful: Verso should use Helmer's switching-costs and process-power framing *within* the "how to win" box — not as a standalone moat claim but as a specification of the mechanism. The cascade is the outer frame; Helmer fills in the winning mode.
**Verdict:** use cascade as the organizing frame in strategic communications; use Helmer vocab (switching costs at seam, process power) as the "how to win" sub-argument.

### [SHARPENS] §2 Non-Goals / Principled Refusal — "playing not to lose" as anti-pattern detector
Martin's "playing not to lose" = defensive roadmaps, feature parity, risk avoidance at every level. This maps directly to Verso's non-goal firewall: any roadmap item that is a response to "LangGraph added X" or "what if someone asks for Y" is playing not to lose. The correct question is always "does this compound our 'where to play / how to win' choices?" If not, it's fear-driven scope, not strategy.
**Verdict:** use "playing not to lose" as the internal litmus for any proposed feature — explicit vocabulary for the non-goal firewall.

### [CONFIRMS] Founder-OS — "strategy is reps, not genius"
> "Great strategists have one thing in common: they just practice."
Directly validates the founder-OS principle that strategic clarity comes from repeated application of the cascade, not from having a brilliant insight once. This makes the cascade a *weekly operating tool* for a solo founder — re-apply it to every major roadmap decision.
**Verdict:** make the cascade a recurring founder-mode practice, not a one-time positioning exercise.
