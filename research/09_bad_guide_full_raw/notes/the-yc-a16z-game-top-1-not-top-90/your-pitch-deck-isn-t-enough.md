## Faithful Digest

### The Central Claim

Troy Kirwin — writing from inside the a16z orbit — makes a structural observation most founders miss: **the investor you pitch is not the decision-maker**. The person you meet still has to sell the deal internally to an investment committee. "It's often not enough for the investor you're talking to to fall in love with the opportunity" — they must convince partners who weren't in the room, who have competing deal flow, and who have limited attention.

A pitch deck is built for live meetings — it tells a story forward with slides. An investment memo is built for internal champions — it stands alone, survives being forwarded, and pre-answers the committee's questions. Most founders never write one. The ones who do lower the friction for their champion dramatically.

### Why Timing Creates a Structural Problem

Kirwin surfaces a non-obvious dynamic: "the decision to bring a company into an investment committee meeting sometimes has less to do with level of conviction and more to do with whether the investor has time to do proper due diligence." In other words, even a high-conviction investor may let a promising deal slip simply because writing the internal memo is work. A founder who hands them a ready-made memo removes that work entirely — the investor can forward it with minimal editing.

This is a **principal-agent cost problem**: the investor's enthusiasm is real, but their bandwidth is finite. The memo is a force-multiplier on enthusiasm.

### Who Benefits Most

Kirwin explicitly names the cases where the memo matters most:
1. **Contrarian or technical markets** — where committee members won't immediately "get it" and need written reasoning
2. **Technical founders weak at verbal communication** — who lose in live meetings despite being right
3. **Fence-sitting investors** — where a well-reasoned written argument can tip a 50/50 into a yes

This framing is important: the memo is not a substitute for a good live meeting; it's insurance for cases where the live meeting is imperfect or the market is non-obvious.

### Recommended Memo Structure (with length targets)

Kirwin gives specific guidance:

| Stage | Length |
|---|---|
| Pre-seed | 3 pages (team + opportunity only) |
| Seed | 5 pages max |
| Series A+ | 8–10 pages max |

**Required sections:**

- **One-liner** — memorable, single-sentence company description (this is the thing the champion quotes to the committee)
- **Team Overview** — placed first because "it's the most important" at early stage; includes why this team specifically can win
- **Problem & Market** — concrete examples preferred over inflated TAM figures; the committee is sophisticated and distrusts unsourced market-size claims
- **Product Overview** — clear wedge strategy + "why now" positioning
- **GTM Strategy** — distribution advantages and customer acquisition approach (not just channel list)
- **Business Model** — revenue approach + evolution pathway (how unit economics improve)
- **Traction** — stage-appropriate metrics; growth signals, not vanity metrics
- **Competitive Landscape** — differentiation theory, not just a 2x2
- **Vision** — five-year company and market evolution
- **12-Month Plan** — capital use plan without specifying exact fundraising amount

### Deck vs. Memo: Complementary, Not Competing

The teaser deck generates meeting interest. The memo provides substantive depth after. Kirwin frames them as different instruments: the deck is a presentation artifact; the memo is a reasoning artifact. They serve different audiences at different stages of the decision process.

## VERSO RELEVANCE

### [SHARPENS] §12 — The One-Liner Discipline Forces a Positioning Test

Kirwin says the memo must open with a "memorable one-sentence company description" because that's what the internal champion quotes verbatim to the committee. This is the same function as the Category Name + POV line in the PRD's §12 positioning work — but the memo context makes it more stringent: it must survive without context, without the founder in the room, without the deck's visual narrative.

**For Verso:** The one-liner must work cold. If it leans on "anti-framework" or "counter-positioning" language, it will confuse non-technical committee members. It must be a category claim + outcome claim, not a contrast claim. Test: can a GP who didn't take the meeting quote it accurately two hours later?

*Tag: [SHARPENS] §12 positioning*  
*Verdict: adopt-now — apply one-liner test to every Verso positioning draft*

### [NEW-IDEA] §12 GTM — The Internal Champion as a Distribution Layer

The memo insight generalizes beyond fundraising: **any decision that involves forwarding your pitch to someone who wasn't in the room** benefits from a standalone reasoning artifact. For Verso's GTM, this maps to the structurally-aware senior engineer who wants to adopt Verso but must sell it to an engineering manager or CTO. The DX-led organic loop (Show HN + X build-in-public) gets the individual to the "I love this" moment — but adoption inside a company requires a champion to carry it forward.

This suggests Verso needs an internal-champion artifact: a one-pager or "why Verso" doc that the champion can forward. Not a marketing brochure — a reasoning document. The existing demo-able wedge (3-line side-by-side, one-flag durable diff) is the *visual* version; the memo is the *written* version.

*Tag: [NEW-IDEA] §12 GTM / distribution*  
*Verdict: adopt-now — design a "why Verso" standalone reasoning doc explicitly for internal champions; this is a cheap, high-leverage channel artifact*

### [SHARPENS] founder_os — The Contrarian / Technical Founder Tax

Kirwin names "technical founders weak at verbal communication" as the highest-value memo beneficiaries. This applies to Verso's founder positioning: if the Verso thesis is correct but non-obvious (harness > framework, eval corpus flywheel, seam collapses tiers), then the live meeting will underdeliver relative to the written case. The memo-first discipline inverts the preparation priority: write the reasoning first, then derive the deck and talking points from it.

This also maps to the "rigorous founder who loses in live meetings" — a specific archetype Kirwin says the memo rescues. Verso's bet is technical and contrarian; the memo format fits the thesis.

*Tag: [SHARPENS] founder_os*  
*Verdict: adopt-now — treat investment memo as the primary artifact; derive deck from it, not the reverse*

### [CONFIRMS] founder_os — "Why Now" Positioning in the Product Overview Section

This confirms the PRD's already-established "why now" framing (AI engineers as the primary persona, 50M software engineers becoming AI engineers, the convergence of reliability requirements and harness primitives). The memo context adds specificity: the "why now" must be concise enough to survive forwarding without the founder explaining it.

*Tag: [CONFIRMS] founder_os*

### [NEW-IDEA] §12 — "Don't Specify the Fundraising Amount" as Positioning Restraint

Kirwin's oddly specific tactical note — include capital use plan but not the exact fundraising amount — has an analog in product positioning: **don't compete on every dimension at once**. The memo discipline of choosing what to include and what to leave for conversation is the same as the PRD's "principled refusal" (§2 non-goals). The restraint signals confidence.

*Tag: [NEW-IDEA] §12 positioning / §2 non-goals*  
*Verdict: sharpen — frame Verso's positioning restraint (what we deliberately don't do) as a signal of confidence, not incompleteness*
