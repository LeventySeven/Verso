## Faithful Digest

This is a16z's internal playbook made public. It is notable for two things: (1) it explicitly teaches founders how to create competitive pressure on investors — including on a16z itself — and (2) it gives a structural analysis of the valuation-vs-terms tradeoff that most founders miss until they've already signed.

### The Core Principle: Capital Sufficiency Over Valuation Optimization

The headline advice: "Raise enough money to put the company in a strong position to achieve the necessary operational and financial milestones and maximize the probability of raising future capital — even if you don't think you'll need to."

The key move is the last clause. Raising more than you think you need isn't profligacy — it's insurance against market shifts. Companies that have runway through the next milestone inflection point can negotiate their next round from a position of strength. Companies that run tight can't.

### Why Capital Is Structurally Abundant Now (Three Forces)

a16z argues that the supply of private capital has permanently increased due to three structural shifts:
1. **Companies staying private longer**: IPO delays push returns into private markets, attracting capital that historically went to public markets
2. **Per-company funding increases**: Individual companies raise billions where they once raised millions
3. **New capital sources**: Corporate strategic investors, sovereign wealth funds, hedge funds, mutual funds, and PE all entered private markets and compete for deal access

Practical implication for founders: you are not capital-constrained in the way founders were a decade ago. The constraint is now deal access (from the investor's perspective), which creates negotiating leverage you should use.

### The Valuation-Structure Tradeoff: The Most Important Thing They Teach

The central insight, expressed as an aphorism: **"You set the price, I'll set the terms."**

Higher valuation ≠ better deal. It may come with structural terms that extract the upside you thought you were protecting. The piece lists specific structural terms to understand:

- **Multiple liquidation preferences** (1.5x–2.0x): Investors get 1.5 or 2 times their money back before common stock sees anything. A 2x liquidation preference on a $20M round means investors get $40M before founders, employees, or earlier investors see a dollar.
- **Senior liquidation preferences**: New investors get paid before earlier investors. This can retroactively harm earlier investors and creates cap table complexity.
- **Participating preferred**: Investors get their liquidation preference AND participate in the remaining upside as if they converted to common. This is the "double dip" structure.
- **Full/partial ratchet**: If the next round prices below the current round, the current investors get additional shares to maintain their effective price. This is extremely founder-hostile in down markets.
- **Redemption provisions**: Forced buyback rights at a specified price. These create a hidden debt-like obligation.

The structural lesson: **terms agreed to in an earlier round become the starting point for the next round.** Subsequent investors will want at least the same protections. Early structural complexity compounds.

### The Two-Part Optimization Framework

a16z recommends evaluating any deal on two axes simultaneously:

**Deal components:**
- Quantifiable: valuation, total dollars raised, option pool refresh size
- Subjective: structural terms, their implications for future rounds, cap table cleanliness

**Source components:**
- Firm reputation and resources (signaling to future investors, help in a down round)
- Partner experience and chemistry (this person is on your board for years)
- Available fund reserves (can this investor lead your Series B?)

The lesson is that optimizing for the quantifiable dimension while ignoring the subjective and source dimensions is a common mistake. A top-quartile valuation from a B-tier investor with bad structural terms is often worse than a modest valuation from a Tier-1 firm with clean structure.

### Engineering Competitive Tension: The Process Mechanics

a16z is explicit about this: founders should run a process designed to create competition. Enough investor conversations to generate multiple term sheets; focused enough to not waste time.

**The leverage shift**: Once a term sheet is signed, investor power increases dramatically. Attempting to renegotiate post-signing signals that something went wrong in diligence, which deters other parties. The conclusion: **optimize terms pre-signing, not post**. Get to the best deal before you sign, because you won't be able to improve it after.

**Investor behavior**: Sophisticated investors know this and price competitively from the start to avoid founders shopping their term sheet. This is important signal: an investor who leads with an extremely high valuation and complex structure is likely less sophisticated than one who leads with a fair price and clean terms.

### When Plans Change: The Implicit Case for Extra Runway

Whether disruption is negative (competitive shock, product delay, macro conditions) or positive (faster growth, earlier-than-expected opportunity), companies will need capital again. This is certain. The question is whether you can raise it on good terms or desperate terms. Extra runway is the difference.

### The Meta-Lesson

> "Optimize for the full relationship over time with aligned partners, not just the headline number today."

This is the synthesis. Fundraising is a repeated game with the same players (the investor who passes on your seed may lead your Series B). The deal structure affects every future interaction. The partner relationship matters more than the pre-money valuation.

## VERSO RELEVANCE

### Founder-OS: The Valuation-Structure Tradeoff as a First-Principles Lesson [CONFIRMS]

The "you set the price, I'll set the terms" aphorism is one of the most useful tactical frames in the guide. Verso's founders need to internalize this before the first term sheet arrives, because the window to negotiate structure is pre-signing, not post. The specific terms to watch (participating preferred, ratchets, senior liquidation preferences) are the ones that will matter most if Verso has a moderate outcome rather than a rocket trajectory.

**Finding:** The structure-over-headline-valuation lesson is pre-requisite knowledge for any sophisticated fundraise. Founders who optimize only for valuation often sign deals they later regret.  
**Tag:** CONFIRMS  
**PRD section:** founder_os  
**Verdict:** confirm — internalize the structural term definitions before any investor process

### Founder-OS: Early Terms Compound — Clean Cap Table as Strategic Asset [NEW-IDEA]

The "terms become the starting point for the next round" insight has a direct corollary for Verso's OSS-first strategy: the cap table and term structure you accept at seed will be visible to every subsequent investor and set their expectations. A clean, simple structure (standard preferred, no ratchets, no senior preferences) signals that the founders understood what they were signing. This is a credibility signal to sophisticated Series A investors.

**Finding:** Cap table cleanliness is a quality signal to future investors. The seed-round term structure should be designed to be legible and founder-friendly because it will be read by every future investor.  
**Tag:** NEW-IDEA  
**PRD section:** founder_os  
**Verdict:** adopt-now — design for cap table legibility from the first instrument signed

### Founder-OS: Engineering Competitive Process as a Skill [SHARPENS]

The explicit advice to run a process that creates competition is non-obvious for first-time founders. The implication for Verso: the launch moment (Show HN, product release, a prominent technical demo) should be timed to create multiple inbound conversations simultaneously. A single conversation is a negotiation; multiple simultaneous conversations are a market.

**Finding:** Investor competitive tension requires simultaneous inbound, which requires a concentrated public launch event. The Show HN launch timing should be coordinated with the fundraising process start, not treated as separate.  
**Tag:** SHARPENS  
**PRD section:** founder_os + §12 (GTM timing)  
**Verdict:** sharpen — time the Show HN launch to initiate or accelerate a fundraising process, not as a purely organic event

a16z's argument that private capital has structurally increased means that the infrastructure layer of the AI stack (which Verso occupies as a framework/harness) is likely to receive continued investment. The moat argument for Verso needs to be defensible against well-capitalized competitors, but the capital environment itself is not a constraint — it's an opportunity. Verso's OSS distribution strategy means the moat has to come from community and eval corpus, not from capital-intensive development.

**Finding:** The structural abundance of private AI capital confirms that Verso's moat cannot be capital-based. It must be community/data/switching-cost-based.

**PRD section:** §1 (bet / moat)

The "full relationship over time" framing reinforces the principle that the first institutional investor is not just a check — they are a multi-year relationship with board influence. For Verso, the right first institutional partner is one who has portfolio companies that would plausibly adopt Verso (AI-native teams, developer tool companies) and who will open those doors actively.

**Finding:** The first institutional investor should be selected for access to Verso's natural adopter community, not just for the check size or valuation.
