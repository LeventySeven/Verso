**Author:** Paul Graham
**Type:** Essay (short)

## Faithful Digest

This is one of Graham's tightest essays — 500 words that compress the founder quality screen to its minimum spec. The goal is to give a single definitive phrase for what makes a good founder.

### The Starting Point: Hapless
Graham starts from the negative pole. "Hapless" doesn't mean unlucky — it means *passive*. "To be hapless is to be battered by circumstances — to let the world have its way with you, instead of having your way with the world." A hapless person has obstacles appear and waits for them to resolve.

The insight: "unfortunate" and "hapless" are different. An unfortunate team loses to a bad referee call. A hapless team doesn't notice the opponent is cheating until after they've lost.

### The Positive Pole: Relentlessly Resourceful
"I was writing a talk for investors, and I had to explain what to look for in founders. What would someone who was the opposite of hapless be like? They'd be relentlessly resourceful."

Two components, both necessary:
- **Relentless:** persistent, doesn't stop
- **Resourceful:** tries different approaches, adapts when the current path is blocked

The running back metaphor: "A good running back is not merely determined, but flexible as well. They want to get downfield, but they adapt their plans on the fly." Pure determination (relentless without resourceful) is what hits granite and stops. Pure resourcefulness (adaptable but not persistent) drifts.

### Why "Resourceful" Alone Is Insufficient
"Not merely relentless. That's not enough to make things go your way except in a few mostly uninteresting domains. In any interesting domain, the difficulties will be novel. Which means you can't simply plow through them, because you don't know initially how hard they are; you don't know whether you're about to plow through a block of foam or granite. So you have to be resourceful. You have to keep trying new things."

This is a sharp epistemological point: novel obstacles require novel responses. Predetermined strategies fail against unknown resistance. You must test the obstacle before you know how hard to push.

### Domain Specificity
Graham explicitly limits the claim: "That sounds right, but is it simply a description of how to be successful in general? I don't think so. This isn't the recipe for success in writing or painting, for example. In that kind of work the recipe is more to be actively curious. Resourceful implies the obstacles are external, which they generally are in startups. But in writing and painting they're mostly internal; the obstacle is your own obtuseness."

The distinction matters: in startup work, the obstacles are external (market, technical, competitive), and resourcefulness is the response to external obstacles. Internal obstacles require a different quality (curiosity, self-knowledge).

### Can It Be Taught?
"After four years of trying to teach it to people, I'd say that yes, surprisingly often it can. Not to everyone, but to many people. Some people are just constitutionally passive, but others have a latent ability to be relentlessly resourceful that only needs to be brought out."

The teachability caveat matters for co-founder selection: not "does this person have the quality now" but "does this person have the latent capacity."

### The Structural Incompatibility with Big Companies
"Being relentlessly resourceful is definitely not the recipe for success in big companies, or in most schools." Big-company success requires a different mix: "some combination of resourcefulness, obedience, and building alliances." Relentless resourcefulness in a big company is actively penalized — it runs into hierarchy, politics, and process.

This creates a natural filtering mechanism: the people who thrive in big companies are exactly the ones who have learned *not* to be relentlessly resourceful.

### The Practical Use
- Self-diagnostic: "If you want to know whether you're the right sort of person to start a startup, ask yourself whether you're relentlessly resourceful."
- Co-founder screen: "If you want to know whether to recruit someone as a cofounder, ask if they are."
- Daily operating principle: "If I were running a startup, this would be the phrase I'd tape to the mirror."

"'Make something people want' is the destination, but 'Be relentlessly resourceful' is how you get there."

Verso's primary persona — the structurally-aware senior/AI engineer — is exactly this person: someone who has been in a large org, has felt the constraint of "obedience and building alliances," and is primed to adopt tools that work the way they think rather than the way the org chart requires. The persona's affinity for Verso is not accidental — it's structural. They are relentlessly resourceful people in environments that penalize that quality.

### [SHARPENS] "You don't know if you're plowing through foam or granite" maps to Verso's eval layer
Graham's epistemological point about novel obstacles — "you don't know initially how hard they are" — maps directly to the agent reliability problem that Verso's harness addresses. Without evals, the builder doesn't know whether a failed agent run hit foam (transient error, easy fix) or granite (fundamental context/prompt/model problem). The harness-as-observability layer is the answer to "you don't know whether you're about to plow through foam or granite" — it makes the obstacle visible before you decide how hard to push.
**Tag:** [SHARPENS]
**Applies to:** §7 product taste — eval/observability layer justification
**Verdict:** sharpen — the foam/granite epistemology is a usable analogy in Verso's documentation for why evals matter before scaling.

### [NEW-IDEA] Haplessness as the failure mode of frameworks that hide complexity
Graham's "hapless" framing — "to be battered by circumstances rather than shaping them" — describes exactly what happens to engineers using frameworks that abstract the agent loop. When the agent fails, the engineer doesn't know why (the loop is hidden), can't fix it (the abstraction doesn't expose the relevant knob), and waits for the framework team to release a fix. That's haplessness as a product outcome. Verso's counter-positioning can be framed in this language: Verso is the tool for engineers who refuse to be hapless about their agent systems.
**Tag:** [NEW-IDEA]
**Applies to:** §12 positioning — anti-framework counter-position; §2 non-goals
**Verdict:** adopt-now — "don't let your framework make you hapless" is a punchy one-liner for launch copy, X threads, and the Show HN pitch. It connects the PG vocabulary to the anti-abstraction counter-position.
