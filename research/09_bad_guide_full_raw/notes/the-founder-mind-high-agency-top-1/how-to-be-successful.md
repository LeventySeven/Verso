**Type:** Essay (medium)

## Faithful Digest

Altman presents 13 principles for achieving outlier success. The essay's organizing claim: reaching the 99th percentile requires both being in the top 90th percentile at something AND working harder than those people. Smart alone isn't enough; hard work alone isn't enough; but compounding them is rare enough to be a sustainable edge.

### 1. Compound Yourself
"I think the most powerful force in the universe is compound interest." This applies not just to capital but to careers. Seek roles with leverage — capital, technology, networks — that let output scale faster than input. "The best jobs are neither the most prestigious jobs nor the best-paying jobs but are instead the ones that give you the best options going forward."

The compounding insight: value growth rate over current value. "I am willing to take as much time as needed between projects to find my next thing. But I always want it to be a project that, if successful, will make the rest of my career look like a footnote."

### 2. Have Almost Too Much Self-Belief
"The most successful people I know believe in themselves almost to the point of delusion." But this must be paired with truth-seeking — the willingness to update when shown to be wrong. "Self-belief must be balanced with self-awareness."

Critical distinction: self-belief that can be deflated by a single critic is not real self-belief. It's approval-seeking dressed as confidence.

### 3. Learn to Think Independently
"One of the most powerful lessons is that original thinking is hard and that you can't teach it in school." The method: generate ideas quickly, be willing to be wrong, use early tests as feedback. "Developing a new idea takes courage — the courage to be different and wrong."

### 4. Get Good at "Sales"
This is framed broadly — not just commercial sales but any situation where you need to get people to believe in something. "All great careers, to some degree, involve learning that authentic belief in what you're doing translates, and is the most powerful way to get people to join you."

Key insight: you can't fake conviction over time. The implication for GTM: if you genuinely believe in the product, you should talk about it constantly.

### 5. Make It Easy to Take Risks
"Look for small bets you can make where you lose 1x if you're wrong but make 100x if you're right." Keep life flexible early. The asymmetry matters: "Most people overestimate risk and underestimate the benefits of taking risks."

Structural advice: don't optimize so hard for current income/status that you can't take the next risk.

### 6. Focus
"Focus is a force multiplier on work." "It is much more important to work on the right thing than it is to work many hours." The anti-distraction case: "Most people waste most of their time on stuff that doesn't matter."

Implication: saying no to almost everything is not conservatism — it's the precondition for excellence on the things you do.

### 7. Work Hard
Reaching the 99th percentile requires both smart AND hard: "You can get to maybe the 90th percentile in your field by working either smart or hard, which is still a great accomplishment. But getting to the 99th percentile requires both."

Important caveats: "Work smart" also matters — direction matters as much as effort. And: "You can also compound yourself in a surprising way—career capital, like financial capital, accumulates."

The early-career argument: "The earlier you build it up, the more compounding you get...hard work has a very high rate of return early in your career."

### 8. Be Bold
"I believe that it's easier to do a hard, important project than an easy, unimportant project." Ambitious projects attract better people and higher energy. "If you are making progress on an important problem, you will have a constant tailwind of people wanting to help you. Let yourself grow more ambitious, and do this as you collaborate with great people."

### 9. Be Willful
Most people don't try to shape the world because they don't believe they can. "Ask for what you want...most people believe they don't have the power to shape the world and therefore don't try. You can do it a lot more than you realize." Persistence allows luck to materialize.

### 10. Be Hard to Compete With
"Most people do whatever most people do. This is great for social cohesion but terrible for generating competitive advantage." The moat: "The best way to be hard to compete with is to be genuinely different — to have a combination of skills, knowledge, and relationships that's hard to replicate."

### 11. Build a Network
Generosity compounds: "The more generous you are with your time and resources, the more you will get back." But quality over quantity: "You want to build a reputation for being honest, smart, hardworking, helpful, and so on."

### 12. You Get Rich by Owning Things
"The biggest economic misunderstanding of my time is that the key to financial success is a high salary." Ownership — of companies, real estate, intellectual property — is how value accumulates. "You need to own equity in something, instead of just selling your time."

### 13. Be Internally Driven
The most important principle: "The most successful people I know are primarily internally driven; they do what they do to impress themselves and because they feel compelled to make something happen in the world...Eventually you will define your success by performing excellent work, rather than by what others think of it."

External validation seeking creates a ceiling: "You will work on consensus ideas and on consensus career tracks. You will care a lot — but other people will too. You will lack the courage to do really interesting exploration."

The 90th-vs-99th distinction reappears here: the 90th percentile can be achieved by chasing external validation; the 99th requires internal drive that persists when external signals are absent or hostile.
