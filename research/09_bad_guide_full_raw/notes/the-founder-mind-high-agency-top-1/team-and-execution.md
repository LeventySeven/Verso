## Faithful Digest

This lecture is the practical operating manual to complement Lecture 1 (Great Ideas, Great Products). Altman's organizing thesis: ideas are worth nothing without execution, and there are "at least 100 times more people with great ideas than people willing to put in the effort to execute them well." The lecture covers co-founder selection, early hiring, team management, firing, and the founder's execution framework — all of it pitched as "unsexy but determinative."

### 1. Co-Founder Selection: The Most Under-Rigorized Decision

"Co-founder relationships are among the most important in the entire company." The statistical evidence from YC: every single one of the top 20 most valuable YC companies has at least two founders. Solo founder funding rate in the YC batch: approximately 1 in 10.

The failure pattern Altman cites directly: "Nine of about 75 companies added on a random co-founder between interview and launch — all nine fell apart in the next year." Random co-founder matching has near-100% failure rates in his observation.

**What to look for — the "James Bond" framework:** Co-founders should be "unflappable, tough, know what to do in every situation, act quickly, decisive, creative, ready for anything." This maps to the YC public phrase "relentlessly resourceful." The framing is specifically NOT "domain expert" — it is temperament and execution character.

**Practical requirements:**
- Known beforehand, ideally for years (college is optimal; large companies like Facebook/Google are "co-founder rich")
- 2–3 founders is ideal; 1 is a disadvantage; 5 is problematic
- Technical co-founder essential for software companies
- Near-equal equity split; decided in first few weeks
- 4-year vesting with 1-year cliff (non-negotiable structural protection)
- Same physical location in early days — "Look at the 30 most successful software startups of all time — try to point to a single example where co-founders were in different locations. It's really tough."

### 2. Hiring: "Try Not To"

Altman's counterintuitive opening: more employees equals higher burn, slower decisions, more complexity. "It sucks to have a lot of employees." The goal is to be *proud* of accomplishing a lot with few people.

Case study: Airbnb spent 5 months interviewing their first employee. They hired only 2 people in their entire first year. Before hiring anyone, Brian Chesky wrote down the company's cultural values and asked candidates: "Would you take this job if you had one year to live?" A bad early hire in the first 5 employees can be fatal; at large companies, mediocre hires "just fall through the cracks."

**The Three-Question Framework:** Altman says he almost never regrets a hire when all three answer yes:
1. Are they smart?
2. Do they get things done?
3. Do I want to spend a lot of time around them?

**Best evaluation method:** Work together on a project for 1–2 days before hiring. Most founders are bad at interviews but excellent at evaluating post-collaboration.

**Experience vs. aptitude:** "Most of the best hires I've ever made have never done that thing before." Experience matters for managing large teams; for early hires, aptitude and mission belief dominate.

**Sourcing:** Personal referrals dramatically outperform other channels. "Most great companies in tech have been built by personal referrals for the first at least 100 employees." Look outside Silicon Valley to avoid concentration of competition.

**Equity:** "Employees will only add more value over time; investors write a check and often don't do much." Guideline: approximately 10% to first 10 employees over 4-year vesting. Founders chronically under-reward employees relative to investors — Altman calls this "totally backwards."

**Reference check hack:** Ask references if the candidate is in the top 5% of people they've worked with. Then ask: "Why aren't you hiring them again?"

### 3. Retention & Firing

**Management fundamentals:** First-time CEOs are "typically terrible" managers. The correction is not a personality transplant — it's awareness of a few patterns:
- Let the team get credit for wins; you take responsibility for failures
- Don't criticize daily; they'll leave
- Give teams autonomy, mastery, purpose (Dan Pink framework)
- Conduct regular 1:1s with clear, frequent feedback

**Firing:** "Every first-time founder waits too long." Fire fast when someone consistently fails across weeks/months. But distinguish between occasional screw-ups (supportive, allow recovery) and "consistently getting everything wrong" (terminate). Special rule: fire immediately for office politics and persistent negativity — these are toxic to startup culture and cannot be managed away.

### 4. The CEO's Five Core Jobs

1. Set the vision
2. Raise money
3. Evangelize (recruiting, partners, press)
4. Hire and manage the team
5. **Set the execution bar** — the most overlooked. Only the CEO can do this. Everything in a startup gets modeled after founders. "You cannot hire a COO and do this while you go to conferences."

### 5. Execution: Two Questions and One Prime Directive

**The Two-Question Framework:** Can you figure out what to do? Can you get it done?

**Focus:** "There are 100 important things competing for attention every day; identify the right 2–3, work on those, ignore/delegate the rest." Most startups work hard on the *wrong things*. "You only get points when you make something the market wants."

**The Prime Directive:** "Growth and momentum are the lifeblood of startups." Altman calls this the single most important operating instruction if he could only give one. A winning team keeps winning; a losing team spirals. Maintain weekly metric reviews. Be suspicious of phrases like "we're not focused on growth right now" or "we're rebranding."

**Recovering momentum:** Long vision speeches don't work. Create small wins. "Sales fix everything in a startup." Facebook 2008: Zuckerberg created a Growth Group focused on small, incremental improvements. It became the most prestigious team in the company.

**Speed as edge:** Best founders respond to email fastest, make decisions fastest, get on planes in marginal situations. The marginal coefficient matters enormously — viral coefficient of 0.99 = flat/dying; 1.01 = exponential forever. "A tiny bit of extra work is the difference between success and failure."

**Competitor noise:** "Don't worry about competitors until they ship a real product. Press releases are easier to write than code." Ford: "The competitor to be feared is one who never bothers about you at all, but goes on making his own business better all the time."

### 6. Intensity and Quality: Both Required

Altman rejects the false trade-off. Apple, Facebook, and Google moved fast and maintained quality simultaneously. The standard runs through the *whole* company — hiring, equipment, process — not just product. "You don't buy people shitty computers if you don't want them writing shitty code."

**Decisiveness:** Indecisiveness is a startup killer. Best founders have a bias toward action — they move fast on seemingly small things, ship continuously, "get new things done every time you talk to them." This is the best predictor of YC batch success Altman knows.

## VERSO RELEVANCE

### Finding 1: The CEO Sets the Execution Bar — Only the CEO Can
**Tag:** [CONFIRMS]  
**PRD Section:** Founder-OS layer  
Altman's fifth CEO job — "set the execution bar" — is the most direct argument for dogfood-first as a founder-OS principle, not a GTM tactic. The harness VERSO's founder uses in their own production agents IS the execution bar. If the founder is running VERSO with eval loops, context discipline, and seam promotion for their own agents, that standard permeates product choices, API design decisions, and credibility with the first 5 vocal leads. You cannot outsource this to a demo.  
**Verdict:** Confirms founder-mode dogfooding as a §1/founder-OS structural choice, not preference.

### Finding 2: The Three-Question Hiring Test — Applied to Adopter Qualification
**Tag:** [NEW-IDEA]  
**PRD Section:** §12 GTM/cold-start  
Altman's three questions (smart? gets things done? want to spend time with?) double as a qualification filter for VERSO's Stage-1 atomic network of 5 vocal production leads. The right leads are not "biggest company" or "most Twitter followers" — they are the AI engineers who are smart, who ship production agents (not demos), and who engage authentically when you show them the harness. The Stage-1 failure mode is recruiting the equivalent of a random co-founder: high status, wrong character.  
**Verdict:** New application — use Altman's three-question filter to qualify Stage-1 network nodes, not just for hiring.

### Finding 3: Growth + Momentum as Prime Directive — The Weekly Metric Check
**Tag:** [CONFIRMS]  
**PRD Section:** §12 GTM/becoming-hunted  
VERSO's "eval-corpus flywheel" is a flywheel precisely because it generates observable momentum: each new production agent that runs VERSO produces data that improves the eval corpus, which improves the harness, which wins the next lead. But this only works if the momentum is *tracked weekly*. Altman's warning about "not focused on growth right now" is exactly the failure mode VERSO's organic/DX-led GTM is most vulnerable to — it feels like you're "building" while the adoption curve is actually flat. Weekly metric review is structural discipline.  
**Verdict:** Confirms §12 — add explicit weekly adoption signal review to the GTM operating rhythm.

### Finding 4: Competitor Noise Is Momentum Destruction
**Tag:** [CONFIRMS]  
**PRD Section:** §12 positioning  
Altman's Ford quote and the "don't worry until they ship real product" rule directly validate VERSO's "competitor is doing nothing" framing. The danger is not LangChain shipping a better harness; the danger is VERSO spending cycles on competitive monitoring and positioning reactions rather than on the eval corpus and the 5 vocal leads. Altman calls this momentum-destroying explicitly.  
**Verdict:** Confirms — the anti-positioning-reaction principle is a *momentum* principle, not just a taste principle.

### Finding 5: Remote Co-Founding Impossibility — VERSO's OSS-Community Analog
**Tag:** [SHARPENS]  
**PRD Section:** §12 GTM/becoming-hunted  
Altman's blunt finding that virtually no successful software company had geographically separated co-founders in the early days is a proxy for the VERSO community question: an asynchronous OSS community is not a substitute for collocated early advocates. The Stage-1 atomic network must be *synchronously engaged* — direct conversation, not GitHub stars and Discord pings. The community layer (async, distributed) is a Stage-2 or Stage-3 phenomenon.  
**Verdict:** Sharpens §12 cold-start — Stage-1 is a synchronous, high-touch relationship with 5 people, not a distributed OSS launch.
