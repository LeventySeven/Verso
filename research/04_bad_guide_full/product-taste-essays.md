## Why Product Sense is the Only Product Skill That Matters (Shreyas Doshi) — https://shreyasdoshi.substack.com/p/why-product-sense-is-the-only-product — read — MUST

**Thesis:** As AI tools commoditize every part of product work (discovery, design, coding, analytics), the only remaining moat is judgment applied *to* AI outputs — what Doshi calls Product Sense. Tool mastery generates zero persistent advantage once everyone has it; the differential is whether you improve on what AI hands you.

**Core frameworks:**

- **Working Backwards Logic Chain**: All competitive companies will reach AI-tool parity within 1–7 years → tool mastery becomes common knowledge → the moat must be judgment quality applied after the tool runs.
- **Two-Tier Competitive Model**: Tier 1 = AI-native teams vs non-AI teams (5–10x efficiency gap). Tier 2 = among AI-native teams, Product Sense is the differentiator.
- **Five-Component Product Sense Decomposition**:
  1. *Strong empathy* — discerning unmet needs AI analysis misses
  2. *Excellent simulation* — seeing future states via domain/tech/customer depth
  3. *Stellar strategic thinking* — right segments, right differentiators
  4. *Great taste* — selecting and explaining the best recommendation
  5. *Creative execution* — conceiving solutions competitors cannot copy
- **Seven Powers in the AI Era**: exclusive data, uniquely correct insight, optimal UX, distribution + bundling, niche business model, regulatory capture, long runway. Product Sense is the upstream capability producing most of them, not one power among seven.

**Tactics:** Stop banking on early AI-tool adoption as career differentiation. Build depth in all five PS components. Use AI to augment judgment, not replace it — the job is improving on already-brilliant AI outputs.

**Numbers:** 80–90% of product professionals still non-AI-native as of 2026. 5–10x efficiency multiple for AI-native teams. 1–7 year window before tool parity arrives.

**Key quotes:**
- "Tools have never been a significant source of alpha in product success and that is not changing with AI tools."
- "The only real long-term career moat for product people is how you can improve on the already-brilliant inputs that AI will provide."

**Why in guide:** Sets the frame for why everything else in this section matters more now — when AI can execute, judgment IS the job.

## How Superhuman Built an Engine to Find Product-Market Fit (Rahul Vohra, First Round) — https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/ — read — MUST

**Thesis:** PMF is not a feeling you wait for — it's a measurable score you engineer upward. Rahul Vohra turned it into a four-step repeatable loop using a single survey question as the leading indicator.

**Core frameworks:**

- **The Sean Ellis PMF Question**: "How would you feel if you could no longer use [product]?" Options: Very disappointed / Somewhat disappointed / Not disappointed. Target: **≥40% "very disappointed"** = PMF threshold.
- **The Four-Step PMF Engine**:
  1. Segment to find supporters → build the High-Expectation Customer (HXC) profile
  2. Analyze feedback: extract what "very disappointed" users love (Q3) + what holds back "somewhat disappointed" (Q4). Disregard "not disappointed" entirely — too far from value.
  3. Build roadmap with **50/50 allocation**: 50% doubling down on what users love, 50% removing blockers
  4. Repeat: track the score as primary KPI, rebuild quarterly
- **High-Expectation Customer (HXC) model** (from Julie Supan): not a broad persona — the most discerning person in your demographic who embodies the product's greatest benefit and drives word-of-mouth. For Superhuman: Nicole, busy professional, 100–200 emails/day, 15–40 sent, seeks zero-inbox 1–3x/week, growth mindset.
- **Paul Graham insight cited**: "Build something a small number of people want a large amount, rather than something a large number of people want a small amount." Maxima in idea space aren't isolated — narrow via data, not intuition.

**Tactics:**
- Survey only users who've engaged with the core product at least twice in the past two weeks. ~40 respondents is directionally accurate at early stage.
- Further segment "somewhat disappointed": split those who value your core benefit (fixable) from those who don't (don't optimize for them).
- Stack-rank roadmap by cost/impact matrix: low-cost high-impact first.
- Run cycle quarterly — PMF degrades as user base expands and becomes more demanding.

**Numbers:**
| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Initial PMF score | 22% (summer 2017) |
| After segmentation only | 32% |
| After 3 quarters of execution | 58% |
| PMF threshold | 40% |
| Superhuman pre-launch wait | 2+ years |
| Roadmap allocation | 50/50 love vs blockers |
| Response time improved to | <50ms (from 100ms) |
| Team size at PMF | 22 people |

**Key quotes:**
- "You can always feel when product-market fit is not happening...word of mouth isn't spreading." (Marc Andreessen)
- "Half your roadmap doubles down on what users love; half addresses blockers." (Vohra's core insight)

**Why in guide:** The most operationally concrete PMF methodology in existence. Copy the four steps directly — survey → segment → 50/50 roadmap → repeat. Turns a vague concept into a weekly KPI.

## Is There Such a Thing as Good Taste? (Paul Graham) — https://www.paulgraham.com/goodtaste.html — read — MUST

**Thesis:** Good taste is real and objective, not merely subjective preference. Proof by reductio: if you deny good taste exists, you must deny good art, which requires denying artists can perform their craft well — an obviously false conclusion. Therefore good taste has objective properties you can learn to see.

**Core frameworks:**

- **Reductio ad Absurdum proof**: The skeptic's position ("it's all just personal preference") implies no one can be a better or worse painter than anyone else — a conclusion no one actually believes.
- **Spectrum of objectivity**: Taste isn't binary (subjective/objective). Works and judges exist on a partial order — not a total ranking, but a real hierarchy. Some works are better; some judges are more discerning.
- **Design-for-audience framework**: Art's quality measures how effectively it operates on human audiences. Like vaccine efficacy — the subjects are variable but the effect is measurable.
- **The craft test**: Graham learned this by studying painting. Observing Leonardo and Bellini, it was "obvious" they were much better — the gap is perceptible to anyone who develops the apparatus.

**Tactics:**
- Practice the craft yourself to see it clearly (Graham's painting study).
- Filter out extraneous influences (artist fame, museum context, social expectation) — evaluate work against acknowledged masters.
- Develop perceptual apparatus: the ability to name *which property* is violated, not just say "I don't like it."

**Numbers:** "A few generations" is the timespan for expert consensus to shift. Eight-year-old painter as baseline comparison for Leonardo.

**Key quotes:**
- "It was obvious that Leonardo and Bellini were much better at it than me."
- "You can't have good actors, or novelists, or composers, or dancers either." (on where denying taste leads)
- "There is definitely not a total order...but there is equally definitely a partial order."

**Why in guide:** The philosophical foundation for the entire "taste is trainable, not innate" argument. Every claim that taste can be built rests here.

## Karri Saarinen's 10 Rules for Crafting Products That Stand Out (Figma Blog) — https://www.figma.com/blog/karri-saarinens-10-rules-for-crafting-products-that-stand-out/ — read — STRONG

**Thesis:** Craft is the sustainable competitive moat in saturated markets where feature parity is inevitable. Linear beat entrenched incumbents by making impeccable quality the non-negotiable — not a nice-to-have.

**The 10 Rules (dense):**

1. **Commit to quality at the leadership level** — leadership must create "a permission structure to build craft into everything." Without top-down commitment, teams default to shortcuts.
2. **Build small, high-quality teams** — "the more people you have, the more opinions and deliberation you introduce, which dilutes quality." Fewer, better people.
3. **Eliminate handoff structures** — siloed PM → designer → engineer disconnects accountability. Linear uses "connected teams in which everyone is responsible for quality."
4. **Resist specialized product teams** — rotating responsibilities keeps ideas fresh, prevents complacency. "Users don't care about your org chart."
5. **Treat specs as baseline minimums, not goals** — the spec is the floor, not the ceiling. Excellence requires exceeding requirements.
6. **Distinguish quality from perfection** — all details must be correct before public release, but perfection pre-launch isn't required. Use beta programs for iterative polish.
7. **Design with specificity and opinion** — "you can only create a great product if you design for someone in particular." Generic products optimized for multiple use cases excel at none.
8. **Reduce scope to increase quality** — when teams struggle with quality, scope reduction beats adding resources. Build one feature exceptionally rather than many features adequately.
9. **Establish values and principles over rigid processes** — empower teams to make contextual decisions. Direct user connections (shared Slack channels) replace process gatekeeping.
10. **Treat data as a crutch, not a compass** — Linear explicitly rejects A/B testing. "You can't expect data — or even people themselves — to tell you how" to surprise users. Build intuition.

**Core mental models:**
- **Quality-Speed Paradox (inverted)**: Reduced scope enables faster iteration toward excellence — quality and speed align when you ship fewer things well.
- **Craft as market differentiation**: In feature-parity markets, craft is the moat — harder to replicate than functionality.
- **Spec Ratchet**: Specs define the minimum. Excellence is what you build above the line.

**Numbers:** Linear's redesign collapsed 98 color variables down to 3. No dedicated search team — rotating responsibility.

**Key quotes:**
- "You have to match that level of capability or quality, otherwise people just don't pay attention."
- "For quality, you need a team that views the spec as the baseline, not the finish line."
- "To provide the best experience, you must surprise users. You can't expect data — or even people themselves — to tell you how."

**Why in guide:** Linear's operating manual in 10 rules. The most actionable articulation of taste-driven product culture from a founder who built it from scratch.

## Product Strategy Means Saying No (Des Traynor, Intercom) — https://www.intercom.com/blog/product-strategy-means-saying-no/ — read — STRONG

**Thesis:** Building great products requires systematic refusal, not accumulation. Saying no is a product capability. Every feature you add costs you forever — in maintenance, in complexity, in product coherence.

**Core frameworks:**

- **The Feature Decision Matrix**: Evaluate every proposal against three criteria: (1) genuinely valuable? (2) fits within product purview? (3) will ALL customers benefit? If any answer is no, the feature likely shouldn't be built.
- **12 Rationalizations for Feature Addition** (all must be resisted):
  1. Strong engagement data (selective analysis)
  2. "It's quick to build"
  3. Single customer retention risk
  4. Make it optional/toggled
  5. Anecdotal customer feedback
  6. Idle engineering capacity
  7. Autonomy promises
  8. Large absolute user demand ("713,000 people want it")
  9. Competitive parity
  10. Market preemption
  11. Executive preference
  12. Speculative transformative potential
- **Death by Preferences**: Making features optional obscures complexity while weakening product definition.
- **Feature Blackmail**: Prioritizing individual customer demands over cohesive strategy.
- **Appeal to the Anecdote**: Extrapolating from insufficient samples.

**Tactics:** For each proposed feature, force the three-question test before any discussion of implementation. The default answer should be no — the burden of proof is on yes. "No one keeps crap ideas in their roadmap. Real decisions require looking at a really great idea and saying we're not going to build it."

**Key quotes:**
- "Building a great product isn't about creating tons of tactically useful features which are tangentially related."
- "No one keeps crap ideas in their roadmap. Real product decisions require looking at proposals and saying 'This is a really great idea...But we're not going to build it.'"
- "There are no small changes."

**Why in guide:** The clearest short case that refusal is a product skill, not a scheduling constraint. Jobs cut Apple from 350 products to 10. This is the philosophical foundation for product focus.

## Linear's Path to Product-Market Fit (Karri Saarinen, First Round) — https://review.firstround.com/linears-path-to-product-market-fit/ — read — STRONG

**Thesis:** Extreme PMF comes from designing for one specific person with no compromises on quality — not from broad positioning, rapid launch, or aggressive acquisition. Quality is the north star; all other metrics follow.

**Core frameworks:**

- **Design-for-Someone Philosophy**: "You should design something for someone. It's hard — impossible even — to design something really good for everyone." ICP: individual contributors (not managers), product builders (engineers, PMs, designers), fast-growing startups.
- **Enablers vs. Blockers**: Two-bucket feature prioritization: (1) Enablers — delight existing motivated users, (2) Blockers — remove barriers preventing ICP adoption. Build enablers and blockers simultaneously; never build anything else.
- **Pre-Work Validation Model**: 12+ months of informal user research before committing full-time. Weekly bar meetings to discuss learnings. Problem validation before building.
- **Founders-as-First-Customer**: Build only what the founders themselves need daily — no compromises.
- **Selective Cohort Onboarding**: Survey-based waitlist, ~10 handpicked users/week. High-signal early users produce high-quality feedback.
- **Opinionated Design Standards**: "I don't think you can build the optimal tool for anything if it's very flexible or endlessly customizable." Defaults over flexibility.
- **Downstream Expansion**: Expand vertically within ICP (issue tracking → project briefs → roadmaps → customer requests), never horizontally to new customer types.

**Tactics:**
- Run informal discovery conversations first: "What's bad about your current tool? How would you improve it? What would increase your productivity?"
- Track retention as the launch signal — only go public after consistently high retention with early cohorts.
- Address critical blockers pre-public launch (Linear switched from Google-only auth to general email signup when that emerged as a blocker).
- Sales process has same quality bar as the product — knowledgeable, high-craft reps.
- Double headcount annually at most; never faster.

**Numbers:**
- 12 months validation before building
- 10,000 emails on waitlist; ~10 users invited/week
- ~1,000 DAU by public launch (~1 year into beta)
- Profitable since 2021 (1 year after public launch)
- 80 people at $1.25B valuation
- 6 months before hiring first employee
- Customers include OpenAI, Ramp, Vercel

**Key quotes:**
- "You should design something for someone. It's hard — impossible even — to design something really good for everyone."
- "Quality is our first principle. Every other metric and decision flows from that."
- "I don't think you can build the optimal tool for anything if it's very flexible or endlessly customizable."

**Why in guide:** The complete Linear origin story — 12 months validation, 1-year private beta, 50 quality-filtered users, profitable in year 1. Every tactical step documented. The counter-narrative to "launch fast, grow fast."

## Product vs. Feature Teams (Marty Cagan, SVPG) — https://www.svpg.com/product-vs-feature-teams/ — partial (403) — MUST

**Thesis:** The single most consequential structural decision in a product org is whether teams are given *features to build* or *problems to solve*. Only teams given problems to solve can achieve the outcomes that make companies great.

**Core frameworks:**

- **The Core Distinction**: Feature teams receive a prioritized roadmap (solutions) from stakeholders/executives. Empowered product teams receive problems and latitude to find the best solution.
- **Four Product Risks**: Every PM/team must manage (1) Value risk — will customers want it? (2) Usability risk — can users navigate it? (3) Feasibility risk — can we build it? (4) Business viability risk — does it work for the business? In empowered teams: PM owns value+viability, designer owns usability, tech lead owns feasibility. In feature teams: value+viability accountability is pushed up to the stakeholder who requested the feature — not the team.
- **Three Team Structures**:
  1. *Empowered product teams* — cross-functional, outcome-accountable, solution-autonomous
  2. *Feature teams* — output-focused, execute assigned features, value risk lives with executives
  3. *Delivery teams* — most common; PM as "backlog administrator," developer-centric
- **Missionaries vs. Mercenaries**: Empowered teams are missionaries (solving customer problems they believe in). Feature teams are mercenaries (building whatever is requested).

**The Key Question for self-assessment**: "Are we being given solutions to build or problems to solve?" If you can't answer that clearly, you're almost certainly a feature team.

**Cagan's diagnostic questions**: Do you own the outcomes? Is your roadmap a list of features or a list of problems? Do you have time for discovery? Are engineers collaborators or builders?

**Key quotes:**
- "Are leaders giving teams solutions to build or problems to solve? That's the key difference."
- Teams must "solve problems in ways customers love, yet work for the business."

**Why in guide:** The canonical short definition of empowered product development — the architectural decision that determines whether a product org can ever be great.

## Opportunity Solution Trees (Teresa Torres, producttalk.org) — https://www.producttalk.org/opportunity-solution-trees/ — read — STRONG

**Thesis:** The OST is a visual framework that keeps product teams accountable to business outcomes while continuously discovering customer needs. It resolves the tension between business value and customer value by structuring discovery hierarchically: outcome → opportunities → solutions → assumption tests.

**Core frameworks:**

- **Three-Tiered Outcome Taxonomy**:
  - Business outcomes: financial metrics (revenue, churn)
  - Product outcomes: customer behavior/sentiment (optimal scope for discovery)
  - Traction metrics: single-feature adoption (too narrow)
- **OST Hierarchy**: Root = desired outcome → Level 1 = opportunity space (top-level moments) → Level 2 = specific opportunities from interviews → Level 3 = solution options → Level 4 = assumption tests
- **Opportunity vs. Solution Distinction**: Opportunities = unmet needs, pain points, desires. Solutions = products/features. Test: "Is there more than one way to address this?" If yes, it's an opportunity.
- **Four-Factor Opportunity Prioritization**: (1) opportunity sizing (count + frequency), (2) market factors, (3) company/strategy alignment, (4) customer importance + satisfaction gap
- **Compare & Contrast Decision Model**: Always evaluate minimum 3 alternative solutions for any target opportunity — research shows this improves decision quality.

**Nine-Step OST Process:**
1. Verify prerequisites (defined target customer, clear outcome, ≥3 customer interviews)
2. Place outcome at root
3. Map opportunity space from all collected stories
4. Select target opportunity
5. Brainstorm solutions for that opportunity only
6. Choose 3 solutions to explore
7. Decompose solutions into underlying assumptions
8. Test riskiest assumptions across all 3
9. Evaluate and decide: iterate, refine, or pivot

**Numbers:**
- 3–4 interviews minimum before initial mapping
- 6–8 interviews = point where opportunity space structure stabilizes
- Update opportunity space every 3–4 interviews (roughly monthly with weekly cadence)
- Grailed achieved 20% LTV lift through opportunity mapping

**Key quotes:**
- "Opportunity solution trees help product trios resolve the tension between business needs and customer needs."
- "When we create business value, we earn the right to serve our customer over time."

**Why in guide:** The operational framework for continuous discovery. Turns "talking to customers" from a phase into a weekly rhythm with an artifact that aligns the team.

## How to Hire a Product Manager (Ken Norton, bringthedonuts) — https://www.bringthedonuts.com/essays/productmanager.html — read — MUST

**Thesis:** Product management is the one organizational role that's temporarily dispensable — which means PMs must earn their value continuously. Great PMs combine intelligence, technical depth, product instinct, cross-functional empathy, influence without authority, and a shipping track record.

**Core frameworks:**

- **The Expendability Principle**: Unlike engineers (who build), salespeople (who sell), or designers (who make things usable), PMs can be skipped for a stretch. This makes the role's value non-obvious — and raises the hiring bar.
- **Six Characteristics of Great PMs**:
  1. Intelligence / raw analytical horsepower
  2. Technical understanding (depth, not necessarily coding)
  3. Product instinct / "spidey-sense" — innate judgment that produces suggestions that seem obvious only after being articulated
  4. Leadership through influence, not authority
  5. Multi-perspective empathy (customer, engineer, sales, exec, marketing)
  6. Shipping track record — actually shipped products, with scars
- **Product Instinct (Three Markers)**: The candidate (1) independently identifies product concerns you're already wrestling with, (2) teaches you something about your own product, (3) introduces you to products/ideas you hadn't encountered.
- **Influence-Without-Authority Model**: PMs lead by persuasion and credibility. Validate this through reference checks with peers and ICs, not just managers.
- **Generalist vs. Specialist dichotomy**: Startups need generalists; large companies can hire for specific roles.

**Interview tactics:**
- "Describe a great product you've discovered recently — why does it appeal to you?"
- "What explains [competitor product]'s success?"
- "What specific improvements would you suggest for my product?"
- "What problems will we face in one, two, ten years?"
- "When do you cut corners to ship faster?"
- Ask analytical questions until confident the candidate outmatches you intellectually.

**Key quotes:**
- "Product management may be the one job that the organization would get along fine without (at least for a good while)."
- "I'll take a wickedly smart, inexperienced PM over one of average intellect and years of experience any day."
- "Great PMs know how to channel different points-of-view. They play devil's advocate a lot."

**Why in guide:** The founding essay of modern PM hiring, still most-cited after 20 years. Defines what product judgment looks like in an interview context — and therefore what you should develop in yourself.

## The Power of Product Thinking (Julie Zhuo, future.com) — https://future.com/product-thinking/ — read — MUST

**Thesis:** Product thinking — knowing what makes a product useful and loved — is a learnable skill, not talent. It precedes design thinking and product vision, and operates as the foundational mindset for zero-to-one development and iteration cycles.

**Core frameworks:**

- **Product Thinking Definition**: "The ability to know what makes a product useful — and loved — by people." Operates as a habit, eye, and mindset. Preceded and guides design thinking and product vision.
- **Three Components**:
  1. *Observation* — your reactions, peers' behaviors, broader market signals
  2. *Inquiry* — deep curiosity into the "why" behind user behaviors
  3. *Iteration* — hypothesis-feedback-improvement cycles
- **Three Levels of Observation**:
  1. Observe yourself: moments of delight vs. annoyance
  2. Observe peers: what friends rave about or complain about
  3. Observe the world: reviews, sentiment, competitive differentiation, reasoning behind opinions
- **Facebook Reactions Case Study (canonical)**:
  - "Dislike" was a top feature request for years, but the word was too ambiguous (disagrees with content? with the person? expresses sympathy?)
  - Team expanded beyond binary, tested animated faces for emotive clarity
  - Ran market tests, let data determine final set
  - Result: 7 emotions tested → 5 deployed (love, anger, sadness, wow, haha, care)
  - This process = observation (users complaining about limited reactions) + inquiry (why dislike is wrong) + iteration (market testing)

**Tactics:**
- Weekly practice: try one new product/feature; have one reflection conversation on how product decisions affect intended audiences.
- Use Julie Zhuo's product critique checklist: onboarding quality, first-minute self-explanation, ease of use, emotional resonance, expectation delivery.
- Compare competing products systematically across: target user persona, core workflow, tone/personality, feature prioritization, onboarding, social mechanics.
- Ask: "At some point, you rely on your instincts for what you believe will work best for your audience." — cultivating that instinct IS the job.

**Numbers:** 7 emotions tested, 5 deployed in Facebook Reactions. Cash App: 76% of 2020 revenues (~$4.6B) from Bitcoin integration — a product thinking call about which job users were hiring Cash App to do.

**Key quotes:**
- "Product thinkers love to discuss their favorite products... they seek to understand the broader question of why a product might or might not work for a broader set of people."
- "At some point, you rely on your instincts for what you believe will work the best for your audience."
- "The job of data is to help you understand the ground truth of what is going on with products, user behavior, and the market."

**Why in guide:** The cleanest definition of product thinking as a trainable skill with a concrete weekly practice. The Facebook Reactions case is the best worked example of product thinking in action at scale.

## What's Your Shape? 12-Competency Model for Product Leaders (Ravi Mehta, ex-CPO Tinder) — https://www.ravi-mehta.com/product-manager-roles/ — read — STRONG

**Thesis:** Product managers cannot and should not excel across all competencies simultaneously. Elite PMs are "spiky" — exceptional in natural strength areas while maintaining baseline competence elsewhere — and build teams whose shapes fit together like puzzle pieces.

**Core frameworks:**

- **The 12 PM Competencies** organized in four domains:
  - *Product Execution*: feature specification, product delivery, product quality
  - *Customer Insight*: customer interview, data analytics, voice of customer
  - *Product Strategy*: product vision, product strategy, product roadmapping
  - *Influencing People*: stakeholder management, team leadership, managing up
- **Four Hemispheres**:
  - West = Product Builders (execution, ship)
  - East = Product Architects (vision, design, draft)
  - North = Product Managers (IC-level work)
  - South = Product Leaders (org-level work)
- **Career Arc**: APM/junior PM → execution mastery → PM/Sr PM → customer insight → GPM/Director → product strategy → VP/CPO → influencing people. Business outcome ownership stays constant at all levels.
- **Spiky Leadership** (from McKinsey/Egon Zehnder 5,000-person study, 2011): High-performing companies developed spiky leaders strong in critical areas, tolerant of complementary weaknesses. Average companies built "well-rounded" leaders — unexceptional results. Key insight: *individuals should be spiky; teams should be well-rounded.*

**Five PM Archetypes:**
1. *Project Manager* — strong execution, gaps in insight/strategy. Builds without knowing what to build.
2. *People Manager* — strong influence, gaps in insight/strategy. Leads without destination clarity.
3. *Growth Hacker* — speed, data fluency, test-driven. Optimizes local maxima, misses global.
4. *Product Innovator* — customer intuition + vision, gaps in execution/analytics. Over-invests in wrong directions without validation.
5. *(Implied balanced leader)* — deliberate multi-competency development.

**Tactics:**
1. Rate yourself on 12 competencies: Needs Focus → On Track → Outperform
2. Plot your shape across four hemispheres
3. Identify archetype
4. Choose: reinforce existing spikes OR develop complementary weaknesses
5. Build teams by hiring shapes that cover your gaps

**Key quotes:**
- "Exceptional people do their best work when encouraged to use and grow natural talents."
- "Individuals should be spiky; teams should be well-rounded."

**Why in guide:** The most rigorous public self-diagnostic for product leaders. Gives @Seventydotleven a framework for identifying where his existing judgment is spiky (likely execution + product strategy) and what he needs to hire or develop around it.

## How to Develop Product Sense (Jules Walter, YouTube/Slack) — https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/product-sense — read — STRONG

**Thesis:** Product sense is ranked the #1 most critical PM skill in surveys yet is the least discussed and most mysterious. Walter demystifies it through four concrete, trainable practices — two for building empathy, two for improving creativity.

**Core definition:** "Product sense is the skill of consistently being able to craft products that have the intended impact on their users." Two pillars: (1) empathy to discover meaningful needs, (2) creativity to devise effective solutions. Empathy without creativity = insight paralysis. Creativity without empathy = irrelevant features.

**Core frameworks:**

- **Product Sense Architecture**: Empathy pillar + Creativity pillar. Both must co-exist.
- **Problem Reframing Model**: How you frame the problem determines solution space. "Reducing bus theft" vs "helping riders feel safer" lead to completely different interventions. Clear, opinionated constraints eliminate weaker options naturally.
- **Observation Hierarchy**: Macro (multiple sessions, 2–4x/month) → Micro (facial expressions, hesitation, confusion) → Meta (your own self-talk when using products)
- **Trend Opportunity Pyramid**: Macro trends (platform shifts: web3, AR/VR, AI; social changes; regulatory) → Micro trends (new APIs, protocol improvements, capability thresholds) → Application: "If this reaches full potential, what opportunities emerge?"

**Four Practices:**

*Empathy:*
1. **Observe user interactions**: Attend 2–4 user research sessions monthly. Ask three questions: "What's the purpose? Who's this for?" / "What actions do you want?" / "What are you thinking? How does it feel?" Document micro-moments: confusion, hesitation, excitement, frustration.
2. **Deconstruct everyday products**: 1–2 hours monthly trying new products. Use Zhuo's critique framework: onboarding, first-minute self-explanation, ease of use, emotional resonance. Compare competing products systematically across persona, workflow, tone, features, onboarding, social mechanics.

*Creativity:*
3. **Learn from product thinkers**: Attend product reviews, document recurring mental checklists great leaders use. Key mentor questions: What prompted conception? What decision points mattered? What approaches were rejected and why? What surprised you?
4. **Stay curious about trends**: Watch developer conferences (Google I/O, WWDC, Meta F8, AWS re:Invent). Follow industry analysts (Ben Thompson). Track VC investment patterns. Meet engineers to understand capability thresholds that unlock new product possibilities.

**Numbers / examples:** Cash App: 76% of 2020 revenues (~$4.6B) from Bitcoin integration. Figma: only became viable when WebGL performance crossed a threshold. Uber: required smartphone GPS proliferation + Google Maps API availability. Slack @channel rooster notification as an example of micro-delight from product sense.

**Signals you're improving:** Notice details you missed before. Anticipate non-obvious problems before product reviews. Generate higher-quality hypotheses in ambiguous situations. Correctly predict impact of changes on metrics more often.

**Key quotes:**
- "The details are not the details. They make the design." (Charles Eames, cited by Butterfield)
- "If you understand why a problem exists and frame it clearly, you will identify enough constraints that only a few solutions will be left."

**Why in guide:** The how-to companion to Doshi's what-it-is. Four concrete weekly practices any builder can start immediately. The Slack and YouTube examples make it tangible.

## Know Your Customers' Jobs to Be Done (Christensen et al., HBR) — https://hbr.org/2016/09/know-your-customers-jobs-to-be-done — partial — OPTIONAL

**Thesis:** Despite unprecedented customer data, 94% of executives are dissatisfied with their organization's innovation performance because they focus on the wrong variable — demographic segments and product attributes — instead of the underlying "job" the customer is trying to accomplish.

**Core frameworks:**

- **Jobs to Be Done**: Customers "hire" products/services to accomplish specific jobs. Jobs have three dimensions: functional (the task itself), emotional (how they want to feel), social (how they want to be perceived).
- **The Milkshake Study (canonical)**: McDonald's discovered that 40% of milkshakes were purchased in the early morning by commuters who wanted something to make the drive less tedious. The job wasn't "satisfy hunger" — it was "give me something to do for 30 minutes while I commute." This led to a completely different product direction (thicker, longer-lasting) than demographics would have suggested.

**Tactics:** Frame discovery around the job, not the product. Ask: "What are they actually trying to accomplish when they use this?" Follow the full causal chain to the real job — the first answer is usually the functional task; keep asking until you reach the emotional and social layers.

**Why in guide:** The milkshake formulation — a 2-minute story that permanently reframes how you listen to customers. The taxonomy (functional/emotional/social job) is the right unit for product discovery.

## Section Body — The Guide's Own Wisdom

### ▸ Know whether you're shipping a feature, a product, or a company
A feature does one thing and gets commoditized in days. A product solves a complete problem and takes months. Only company-level moats compound: a data flywheel, distribution, lock-in. "AI that drafts X" is a feature any wrapper ships next week. The lead-gen *outcome* loop is where the company lives.

### ▸ Make AI drive revenue, not just cut cost
The strongest AI companies pass the business-model-reinforcement test: more usage makes the customer *more money*, so there's no adoption ceiling. A cost story caps out at the headcount you displaced. Frame so that more agent activity means more qualified pipeline and more closed revenue for the customer — then their spend on you is trivial against the upside, and you've removed the ceiling.

### ▸ Taste is objective — train the perceptual apparatus
Graham's point: you don't say "I don't like it," you name *which property* is violated. Karri Saarinen looked at Jira and saw mess where everyone else had normalized it; that intolerance, backed by domain depth, is what built Linear. Use a huge number of products *deeply*, not casually, until you can diagnose precisely what's wrong.

### ▸ Product strategy is a principled refusal model
Des Traynor's 2013 line still holds: every feature you add costs you forever. Saying no is a product capability, not a scheduling constraint. Jobs cut Apple from 350 products to 10. The discipline is refusing the ten plausible integrations that would each split attention and dull the core loop.

### ▸ Be your own CPO — founder-mode on the details that matter
Brian Chesky dismantled Airbnb's PM layer and runs product review himself every week. Founder-mode means engaging past the org chart on decisions that set product coherence, not delegating taste to a layer that will average it toward safe.

### ▸ Operationalize PMF instead of waiting to "feel" it
Rahul Vohra turned PMF into a number: "how would you feel if you could no longer use this?" Track the >40% "very disappointed" segment, then build only for them. Pair with Cursor's rule: watch *paid power-user* retention, not MAU.

### ▸ Start from the experience, work backward to the tech
Jobs at WWDC 1997: start with the customer experience and work backward to the technology, not the reverse. The AI-slop trap is the inverse: gradient-perfect UI that is tonally dead. Lancelot/LangGraph plumbing is a means; what the user feels is the product.

**What this section makes you better at:**
- Diagnosing product quality at the property level, not the vibe level
- Building a measurable PMF engine rather than waiting for the feeling
- Defending product focus against every plausible distraction
- Running product review in your own head every week without a PM layer

## → Top Actionable Takeaways for @Seventydotleven

1. **Deploy the Superhuman PMF engine immediately.** Survey users with Sean Ellis's one question. Set the >40% "very disappointed" target as your primary KPI. Build the HXC persona. Allocate 50% roadmap to doubling down on what they love, 50% to removing their blockers. Run quarterly. This is the single most concrete, stealable framework in the section.

2. **Name which property is violated, not that you don't like it.** When reviewing any product decision — yours or a competitor's — force yourself to name the specific broken property (too slow, wrong mental model, missing emotional resonance on state X). "I don't like it" is not a product call.

3. **Frame everything as feature vs. product vs. company.** Before building any capability: is this a feature (commoditized in weeks), a product (complete problem, months), or a company-level moat (data flywheel, outcome ownership)? The answer determines whether it's worth prioritizing or defending.

4. **Treat the spec as the floor, not the ceiling.** After any implementation decision: ask what would make this excellent, not just functional. Saarinen's rule — specs define the minimum, excellence lives above the line.

5. **Refuse features with the Traynor three-question gate.** Every proposed feature must pass: (1) genuinely valuable, (2) within product purview, (3) benefits ALL customers. One failure = no. Build the habit of defaulting to no — the burden of proof is on yes.

6. **Build for the most discerning specific person, not the average.** Linear's entire $1.25B outcome traces to designing for one ICP (individual contributors at fast-growing startups) with no flexibility concessions. Opinionated over flexible, always.

7. **Use OST as your weekly discovery artifact.** Map: outcome → opportunities (customer unmet needs) → solutions → assumption tests. Update every 3–4 interviews. Never respond to the loudest feedback — respond to the highest-value opportunity in the tree.

8. **Know your PM shape.** Plot yourself on Mehta's 12-competency framework. Identify which two or three competencies are your natural spikes. Stop trying to be well-rounded — hire/partner to cover gaps, double down on spikes. For a CTO-type founder: likely strong on Product Execution and Product Strategy; likely gaps in Influencing People at scale and Customer Insight depth.

9. **Weekly product sense practice: try one product + one reflection conversation.** Zhuo and Walter agree this is the minimum muscle-maintenance routine. The compounding happens over 6–12 months of doing this every week without exception.

10. **In the AI era: your job is improving on what the model hands you, not using the model.** Doshi's frame is load-bearing here. If the model generates a product recommendation, the value you add is the judgment call on whether that recommendation is right — not the prompt that produced it.
