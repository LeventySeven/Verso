## Competition is for Losers (Peter Thiel) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3Fx5Q8xGU8k — transcribed — MUST

**Thesis:** The only two types of businesses are perfect monopolies and perfectly competitive. The apparent difference looks small because monopolists lie about having monopolies and non-monopolists lie about having differentiation. The real difference is enormous. Aim for monopoly; competition is for losers.

**Frameworks:**
- **X × Y Framework:** Value = X dollars created × Y% captured. X and Y are *independent*. Airlines create massive X (societal value) but have near-zero Y. Google creates less X but captures 20-30% → Google is worth far more. Science historically has Y ≈ 0% — inventors don't get rewarded.
- **Monopoly Characteristics:** Proprietary technology (ideally 10x better on some key dimension — PayPal 10x faster than checks, iPhone = first smartphone that worked), network effects (start hard, compound with scale), economies of scale (software: marginal cost = 0), brand.
- **Last Mover, not First Mover:** First mover has 1/3 of a pawn advantage. What matters is being the *last* company in a category. Microsoft = last OS for decades, Google = last search engine. "Begin by studying the endgame." 75-85% of a tech company's value comes from cash flows in 2024 and beyond — durability dominates growth rate.
- **Small Markets → Expansion:** Start with markets so small people dismiss them. PayPal: started with 20,000 eBay power sellers, hit 25-30% market share in 2-3 months. Facebook: 10,000 Harvard students, 0% to 60% in 10 days. Amazon: started with books. eBay: started with Pez dispensers. The business school analysis always says too small → they were right that the market was small and wrong that it couldn't grow.
- **The Lying Problem:** Monopolists describe their market as huge (Google says it's in "technology" not "search"). Non-monopolists describe theirs as tiny intersections (we're the only British restaurant in Palo Alto). Ask: what is the *real objective market*, not the narrative?
- **Vertical Integration as Moat:** Tesla and SpaceX — no single 10x breakthrough in battery/rocketry, but integrated all pieces more vertically than competitors. Eliminating rent-seeking subcontractors is the moat.
- **The Psychological Trap:** Competition is mimetic — we seek it for validation. "The battles were so ferocious because the stakes were so small" (Kissinger on Harvard faculty). Competition makes you better at competing but causes you to stop asking what's truly important. "Don't always go through the tiny little door that everyone's trying to rush through — maybe go around the corner and go through the vast gate that no one's taking."

**Tactics:**
- When evaluating an idea: ask what is the *actual* market, not the narrative market.
- Lean startup is overrated. Great companies weren't built on customer surveys. Founders who succeeded often suffered "mild forms of Asperger's" — not easily deterred by what people told them.
- Complexity of vertical integration is underexplored and undervalued.

**Key Numbers:** Airlines: $195B domestic revenue, ~0% cumulative profit over 100 years. Google: $50B revenue, ~3.5% of global advertising. At PayPal in 2001: 75% of DCF value came from post-2011 cash flows.

**Quotes:**
- "Capitalism and competition are antonyms. A capitalist accumulates capital. Perfect competition competes it all away."
- "All happy companies are different because they're doing something unique. All unhappy companies are alike — they fail to escape the essential sameness that is competition."
- "Don't always go through the tiny little door everyone's rushing through. Go through the vast gate no one is taking."

**Why in guide:** The deepest single framework for problem selection — teaches @Seventydotleven to stop competing and start picking. Especially relevant when evaluating whether Lancelot or any new project is monopoly-shaped or competition-shaped.

## Jensen Huang — View From The Top — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lXLBTBBil2U — transcribed — MUST

**Thesis:** Build around a core belief about what problem matters, find EIFs (Early Indicators of Future Success) before the market exists, go back to first principles constantly, and create conditions for your employees to do their life's work.

**Frameworks:**
- **EIFs (Early Indicators of Future Success):** KPIs are results. What you need are leading indicators that you're going in the right direction *before* market exists. For NVIDIA, "is this important work? Would it happen without us?" was the EIF. Not a financial forecast — the importance of the work is the early indicator of a future market.
- **"If we didn't do it, would it happen without us?"** If yes → you don't need to do it. The company should be lazy about things others can do. Only do things that won't happen without you. This paradoxically creates the most purpose.
- **First Principles Reset:** NVIDIA's DirectX incompatibility crisis: instead of giving up, found the OpenGL manual at Fries Electronics for $68, read it, then reimplemented the graphics pipeline from scratch better than anyone had. Lesson: "You can learn how something can be done, go back to first principles, and given current conditions, how would you redesign this whole thing?"
- **"How hard can it be? You're probably one arXiv paper away."** Attitude toward unknown domains. Read the paper. Learn the system. Redesign it from scratch for today's conditions.
- **Flat + Transparent Org:** 50 direct reports. The reason CEOs have few direct reports is they treat information as power. Jensen's view: your position should reflect your ability to reason, lead, and inspire — not the information you hoard. "I don't believe in an environment where the information you possess is the reason for your power."
- **When stock drops 80%:** Go back to the core. "Did the physics change? Did the gravity change? Did any of the things that led to our decision change? If not, you change nothing. You keep going."
- **Look backward from the future:** "I go forward in time and look backwards. I imagine NVIDIA making a unique contribution to computing and work backwards." Not: where am I now going forward. But: where am I going, and what does the path look like from there to here?
- **Every job is beneath no one:** Jensen was a dishwasher, then a busboy. No task is beneath him. "You can't show me a task that is beneath me." The lesson is about staying connected to operational reality.

**Tactics:**
- Teach people *how you reason*, not just what to decide. Show your reasoning transparently so the company internalizes it.
- Create an org where everyone understands the context → they make smart decisions on your behalf every day.
- Use EIFs when building toward markets that don't yet exist: "Does this advance science? Is this important work? Would it happen without us?"

**Key Quotes:**
- "I want the company to be lazy about things other people can do. We should go select the things that if we didn't do them, the world would fall apart."
- "Have a core belief. Gut check it every day. Pursue it with all your might. Pursue it for a very long time. Surround yourself with people you love."
- "When your share price drops 80%, you go back to the core. If none of the things that led to your decision changed, you change nothing. You keep going."

**Why in guide:** Jensen's framework for conviction under uncertainty and building toward markets that don't yet exist is directly applicable to Lancelot and skale-brain. The EIF concept is immediately usable.

## Before the Startup (Paul Graham) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f4_14pZlJBs — transcribed — MUST

**Thesis:** Six counterintuitive truths about startups that contradict your instincts. The core: you need expertise in your *users*, not in startups. Live in the future, notice what's missing, learn powerful things.

**Frameworks:**
1. **Startups are counterintuitive** — trust your instincts about people but not about business situations. If someone seems impressive but gives you misgivings, the misgivings are correct.
2. **You need expertise in users, not startups** — Zuckerberg succeeded because he understood his users, not because he knew startup mechanics. Facebook was first incorporated as a Florida LLC. "Growth hacks" = translate mentally as [expletive]. The answer is always "make something users really love and tell them about it."
3. **Gaming the system stops working** — Startups are one of the rare places where you cannot fake it. Users are like sharks: they can't be fooled. Faking works with investors for maybe two rounds, but you're playing a confidence trick on yourself.
4. **Startups are all-consuming** — Larry Page has been running at full speed since 25. It's like pressing a button that irrevocably changes your life. Don't start at 20 — explore, travel, work on interesting things, let your brain become the type that notices startup ideas unconsciously.
5. **You can't tell in advance** — No one, not even PG after 9 years of YC, can reliably predict who will be great. The swaggering recruits are no more likely to be tough than the quiet ones. Unless you're terrified, just try.
6. **Don't try to think of startup ideas** — Consciously trying to generate startup ideas produces bad plausible-sounding ones. Instead: turn your brain into the type that has startup ideas *unconsciously*. Yahoo, Google, Facebook, Apple were all side projects. How: learn a lot about things that matter, work on problems that interest you, with people you like. "Live in the future and build what's missing."

**The Two-Word Advice:** "Just learn." The component of entrepreneurship that matters is domain expertise. Larry Page is Larry Page because he was genuinely interested in search. "Starting a startup is merely an ulterior motive for curiosity."

**Key Tactical Points:**
- Culture is "not architecture but gardening" — plant seeds, pull weeds.
- Early hires = almost like founders, must be self-motivated, can't need management.
- Best way to raise money: make the startup actually grow fast, then simply tell investors.
- The best startup ideas always look crazy at first: Twitch.tv → "people watching other people play video games?"

**Quotes:**
- "The way to get startup ideas is not to try to think of startup ideas... Turn your brain into the type that has startup ideas unconsciously."
- "Gaming the system may work if you go to work for a big company. In startups, there's no boss to trick. There's only users, and users only care whether your software does what they want."
- "The ultimate advice for young would-be startup founders, reduced to two words: just learn."

**Why in guide:** The strongest counter to the trap of performing entrepreneurship instead of doing it. The "live in the future" framework is exactly how @Seventydotleven should think about what to build next.

## How Brian Chesky Is Redesigning Airbnb for the AI Era — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eURcW5_uS60 — transcribed — MUST

**Thesis:** Founder mode is not about control — it's about contact with reality. AI era requires going even deeper into details, removing abstraction layers, and making problems as small as possible before scaling. Build intrinsically, not for agilation.

**Frameworks:**
- **Founder Mode Origin Story:** By 2019 Airbnb had 7,000 employees. Chesky felt like "in a car without a steering wheel." No one was in control. Pandemic hit, lost 80% of business in 8 weeks. Took total control. Reviewed every single thing for 2-3 years, 100hrs/week. "Before I empower people, I need to know what's going on."
- **Golf Instructor Model of Management:** Most founders develop bad habits first, then try to fix them. Correct approach: be totally hands-on at the start (like a golf instructor watching every swing), teach everything you know, let go gradually, repeat with each new team. "Everyone does the opposite — they let them figure it out and then intervene, but they already have the wrong muscle memory."
- **11-Star Exercise:** Five stars = nothing went wrong. 6★: wine and fruit waiting. 7★: limo, surfboard, city tour. 8★: parade with elephant. 9★: Beatles welcome. 10★: Elon Musk takes you to space. "You push to the absurd so that 6 or 7 stars no longer seems crazy." The gap between 5★ and 6★ is the difference between you and a competitor. Go beyond the edge of reality, then work backwards.
- **Hawaii System (lean teams):** Small elite team (10-12 people), crawl→walk→run→fly approach, measure everything. Year 1: $200M equivalent internal revenue. Year 2: $400-500M. Now: >600bps on $134B GMV. Same methodology applied to pricing → then to other verticals. "One to ten to many: pilot one market, get it working, go to ten, industrialize."
- **Simplicity = Distillation, not Removal:** From Heroki Asai (Steve Jobs's creative director): "Simplicity is not removing things. Simplicity is distilling something so fundamentally that you understand its essence." Steve's quote: "Design is the fundamental soul of a man-made creation that reveals itself through subsequent layers." = great design distills to essence.
- **Score Takes Care of Itself:** Bill Walsh's 49ers principle: don't focus on winning, focus on getting all inputs perfect. John Wooden's first hour of practice: how to put your socks on. "How you do anything is how you do everything." Applied at Airbnb: stopped focusing on growth, focused on making everything perfect → if inputs are right and you don't grow, you have the wrong inputs.
- **Agilation trap:** Success became a scorecard. Chesky realized he was seeking agilation (external validation/status) not intrinsic motivation. "Agilation is like a cup with a hole at the bottom. You fill it thinking it's love, but it keeps emptying." Solution: "I stopped trying to be successful and just went back to the basics. Make stuff. Make it for yourself."
- **Focus on what you want to DO, not what you want to BE:** "If I focus on who I want to be — a giant tech founder — then there's always a way to fail. If I focus on what I want to make, there's no way to fail." (Per Obama's advice.)
- **AI Founder Mode:** Fewer meetings, more async. Fewer management layers (Catholic Church: 2000 years on 4 layers). Pure people managers have no value in the AI era. Everyone must be hybrid: "you manage people through the work, not the people." Two types who won't survive: pure people managers and those who refuse to change.
- **Atomic Unit Shift:** Airbnb's next phase = shift the atomic unit from "a home" to "a person." Build the richest identity/preference profile on the internet. Identity → then expand to 50 services (like Amazon from books to everything).

**Key Numbers:** Project Hawaii team: 10-12 people → $200M Y1, $400-500M Y2, now 600bps on $134B GMV. Pandemic: lost 80% of business in 8 weeks.

**Quotes:**
- "No one is born a good CEO. The job of CEO is completely counterintuitive and almost all of your intuition about what to do is wrong."
- "Great leadership is hiring great people and trusting them to know what to do. Well, how do you know they're great if you're not auditing what they're doing?"
- "Control and power is not zero sum. If I have more power and control, I can give you more power and control."
- "Don't focus on who you want to be. Focus on what you want to do. That way there's no way to fail."

**Why in guide:** The most operational account of founder mode available. The 11-star exercise, golf instructor model, Hawaii system, and intrinsic vs. agilation framing are all immediately deployable.

## How to Operate (Keith Rabois) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6fQHLK1aIBs — transcribed — MUST

**Thesis:** Your job is to maximize output of your organization. The best metaphor: you are an *editor*, not a writer. Build the engine, don't be the engine.

**Frameworks:**
- **Editor Metaphor:** Writers do the work. You edit. Four editing tasks: (1) simplify/eliminate — force everything to 140 characters of clarity; (2) ask clarifying questions to identify what actually matters; (3) allocate resources (like reassigning reporters to stories); (4) ensure consistent voice — everything should sound like one person wrote it.
- **Andy Grove's High Output Management:** Your job = maximize output of your organization + organizations that surround it. Measure outputs not inputs. "You can improve performance by 30-50% by eliminating unnecessary steps."
- **Barrels vs. Ammunition:** Adding people usually doesn't increase throughput. What you need are *barrels* — people who can take an idea from conception all the way through shipping while bringing others with them. You can only shoot through the number of barrels you have. 1 barrel → 2 barrels = 2x output. Ammunition is abundant; barrels are scarce. How to find barrels: give small trivial responsibilities first. Intern who gets smoothies delivered on time at 9pm cold every night → give bigger scope. Watch: who do people go to desks of without being asked? Those are your barrels.
- **Task Relevant Maturity (Grove):** Management style should vary by employee. For someone new to a task → high monitoring, detailed instruction. For someone proven → delegate completely. "It's a feature, not a bug, if half your references say you micromanage and half say you give lots of autonomy."
- **One Thing Per Person (Thiel at PayPal):** Every person has exactly one thing assigned. Refused to discuss anything else. "I will not talk to you about anything except this one thing." Forces solving A+ problems (high impact but difficult) rather than B+ problems (easier but less important). People naturally procrastinate their hardest problems and solve the second and third instead. One-thing focus forces engagement with the real bottleneck.
- **Conviction × Consequence Matrix:** High conviction + high consequence → you cannot let others make the mistake, explain your reasoning, override. Low conviction + low consequence → delegate completely, let people learn. Use Social Capital sparingly on "you're the boss" situations.
- **Dashboard First Principles:** Founder drafts the dashboard. What does business success look like? What are the inputs? Success metric: what fraction of employees use it every day? Should be close to 100%.
- **Anomaly Detection over Expected Behavior:** PayPal: 54 power sellers hand-wrote "pay me with PayPal" into eBay listings. First reaction of exec team: kick them out. David Sacks: "I think we found our market." LinkedIn: 25-30% of all clicks went to users' own profiles. Made no sense. Max Levchin: "vanity." People were looking in a mirror. Insight: watch for anomalies, not confirmation of expected behavior.
- **Score Takes Care of Itself (Bill Walsh):** Details cascade. Walsh took over 49ers when they were 2-14. First action: teach receptionists how to answer phones. Three-page memo. If the organization executes everything exactly right → receivers run routes at 7.5 yards, not 7 or 8 → the whole team performs at peak.
- **Pairing Metrics:** Never measure one thing in isolation. Risk team measured on fraud rate → treats all users as suspects → false positives explode. Pair fraud rate with false positive rate. Pair hiring volume with quality of hires.

**Tactics:**
- Transparency: All employees get access to all metrics. Board deck shared with entire company (strip compensation if needed). Glass walls on conference rooms — people worry less when they can see who's in meetings.
- Office space: Do it yourself. Never outsource. The environment dictates culture and decision-making more than almost any other factor.
- Every startup is a cult — hard to build a cult in shared office space.

**Quotes:**
- "You can change the world in 140 characters. There's no reason you can't run your company the same way."
- "Most great people are ammunition. What you need are barrels."
- "Peter Thiel would insist every single person could only do exactly one thing. We all rebelled. But the insight is that without it, everyone solves B+ problems instead of A+ ones."

**Why in guide:** The most tactical operations framework in the canon. The editor metaphor, barrels/ammo distinction, and one-thing rule translate directly to how @Seventydotleven should structure Lancelot's early team.

## How to Manage (Ben Horowitz) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uVhTvQXfibU — transcribed — MUST

**Thesis:** Management decisions have effects far beyond the person you're talking to. You must see every decision through the eyes of *everyone in the company*, not just the person in the room.

**Frameworks:**
- **The Whole Company Lens:** Every critical decision must be understood from all points of view — not just yours or the person you're talking to, but every employee not in the room. The core error: thinking you're making a decision about one person when you're actually setting policy for the whole company.
- **Demotion Case Study:** Exec is working 16-hr days, everyone likes him, but he's over his head technically. CEO wants to demote rather than fire. Horowitz's counter: that exec has 1.5% equity. Your engineers have 0.1-0.2%. What does it signal to every engineer when that underperforming exec keeps 1.5%? What happens to respect for him? The equity problem makes demotion worse than firing.
- **Raise Request Case Study:** Employee asks for raise. Giving it feels good for the one who asked. But the employee who didn't ask and is performing *better* now sees: "Raises go to those who ask, not those who perform." Every employee now has a fiduciary duty to their family to ask for raises constantly. Solution: **formal process**. Make it known: I have a periodic review process. I evaluate everyone's work and give raises on that cycle. Lobbying is heard but the decision is made by process. This protects everyone — no one wonders if it's politics or favoritism.
- **Equity window extension (Sam's blog post critique):** Old 90-day exercise window is a product of APB 25 accounting law (repealed 2004). Under old law, extending to 10 years was expensive accounting-wise. Now the law has changed — 10-year exercise windows are the right move. Horowitz endorses Sam's recommendation with the caveat that you need to understand *why* the old rule existed before changing it.
- **Lincoln / See Through Everyone's Eyes:** Horowitz's "history's greatest practitioner" = Lincoln. Emancipation Proclamation timing, cabinet management, keeping generals in place while managing public perception — all examples of seeing decisions through every constituency's eyes simultaneously before acting.

**Key Tactical Points:**
- Never give off-cycle raises based on ask alone — it destroys the meritocratic signal.
- Do formal compensation reviews on a cadence (quarterly or semi-annually for fast-moving companies).
- When making any people decision, write out what every category of employee will think when they hear about it.

**Quotes:**
- "When you're making a critical decision, you have to understand how it's going to be interpreted from all points of view — not just your own."
- "If you give raises when people ask, you will have a lot of people asking for raises. That is called encouraging behavior."
- "The right answer on raises is to be formal. It saves your own culture."

**Why in guide:** Teaches the "second-order people management" skill that most founders learn only after burning through early teams. Critical for @Seventydotleven before first hires.

## How to be a Great Founder (Reid Hoffman) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dQ7ZvO5DpIw — transcribed — MUST

**Thesis:** Great founders aren't superpersons who excel at everything. They have a few spikes, know which networks matter for their specific problem, and take the contrarian-and-right bet. Entrepreneurship is "jumping off a cliff and assembling an airplane on the way down."

**Frameworks:**
- **Spikes, Not Panopticon:** The myth of the Steve Jobs/Bezos/Gates superperson is misleading. Real great founders have a couple of unique edges relevant to their specific problem. You're not looking for someone who scores 10/10 on all dimensions. Complement with co-founders.
- **Co-founders > Solo Founders (usually):** "Two or three is better than one." Different skills compensate. Can handle more problem dimensions simultaneously. A co-founder divorce a year in is often fatal — it requires high trust, ideally years of prior relationship.
- **Location = Network Signal:** Go to the networks essential to your problem. Silicon Valley is excellent for software/network/marketplace startups but *bad* for sales-intensive businesses (Groupon needed Chicago's sales culture). Fashion startups don't belong in SV. "Great founders realize it isn't about being a superperson who can do this anywhere — it's about going where the strongest networks are."
- **Contrarian-and-Right:** Being contrarian is easy. Being contrarian and right is hard. Test: "Can you identify a smart person who disagrees with you for intelligible reasons?" If not, you're not actually contrarian. Real contrarian ideas have smart experts who would argue against them.
- **Scale Thinking from Day One:** Hoffman's key differentiator for great founders vs. good ones: great founders think about scale from day one. Not "how do I get 100 users" but "what is the system that gets me 10M users and what are the constraints?" This shapes architecture, hiring, and prioritization differently.
- **Network Leverage:** LinkedIn insight: your network is your leverage. For Hoffman, company networks are leverage multipliers — every hire you make brings their network, every investor brings their portfolio company connections.

**Quote:**
- "Entrepreneurship is jumping off a cliff and assembling an airplane on the way down."
- "Contrarian is always relative to an audience. A good contrarian idea is one that consumer internet experts think isn't good yet — because you know something they don't."

**Why in guide:** Reframes the founder self-concept away from superperson toward "a few spikes + right network + contrarian bet." Directly useful for calibrating expectations about what it takes to start Lancelot's commercialization.

## Scale AI & the Startup Hunger Games (Alexandr Wang) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CEebe451GZs — transcribed — MUST

**Thesis:** Build conviction from first principles, not from trends. The defining founder trait is creative problem-solving on unglamorous operational problems, with extreme work ethic. In AI, 80-90% of what people say is noise — develop independent beliefs.

**Frameworks:**
- **"Already dead, find out in 3 years" (Hunger Games):** "The most terrifying thing was that you could already be dead but might only find out in three years." Most startups die that way. Channel the anxiety into doing things. Keep moving, keep building signal.
- **Living in the Future (PG's framework applied):** Scale's original idea: "In the future, human computation will be orchestrated dynamically via API the way we orchestrate compute today. That API doesn't exist." Bought scale.ai domain the same night. Launched on ProductHunt. 6 months of wandering. One big client → traction.
- **Irrational Self-Belief as Success Ingredient:** Referenced Palantir's "God's greatest software" sales culture. "It's actually part of the success recipe — you need some kind of irrational self-belief to compete in the marketplace." Rational observers think it's obnoxious. But it creates a self-reinforcing loop of recruiting, product decisions, going the extra mile.
- **Unsexy = Moat:** Scale's 10x spike: "Creative problem-solving on the least sexy problems you can imagine." Operations at scale (hundreds of thousands of contributors worldwide) treated with the same rigor as a math Olympiad problem. Culture metric: "Think about net economic impact of the problem you're solving, not its technical difficulty." Lots of very economically valuable problems are not technically difficult — they're just hairy. That's where most startup opportunities are.
- **Facebook's Promotion Culture:** Promoted the most productive engineers, not those who tackled the hardest problems. Senior engineers would be confused: "Why is this person getting promoted for reducing log errors?" The answer: "Because that shit needed to be done and they did it." Net economic impact > technical prestige.
- **Data Wall is Real:** Pre-training limits are real. Progress now comes from post-training, which is bottlenecked by specialized, high-quality human-generated data, not compute. "Synthetic data hasn't panned out — you lose the real richness of the data distribution." The path forward: scale data in tandem with compute.
- **AI as Geopolitical Race:** First replication of OpenAI's inference-time compute scaling came from DeepSeek (Chinese lab, open-source). This means there is no research gap between US and Chinese top labs. The key leverage is export controls on chips (China has ~1/100th the high-end GPUs of the US). The question for 2025+: will export controls hold under Trump? And which AI stack do countries around the world adopt — US or Chinese?
- **Agent product design > model quality:** "The biggest blocker for the watershed agents moment is product design, not model quality. The models are already good enough. Nobody's figured out how to break them out of the chat paradigm into something baked into fundamental workflows. That's the biggest startup opportunity in 2025."
- **Develop independent beliefs:** "Most people in San Francisco don't really believe anything and get all their ideas off Twitter and parties. Your mandate is to not be one of those people." At every point, what you build will be popular or unpopular. Your job is to weather the crazy storm. "You're never as bad as the world tells you, and you're never as good as the world tells you." — Mark Zuckerberg

**Quotes:**
- "If you think about competition, just be better. Just be better."
- "Most people are confident and wrong in AI. There's a large premium in figuring out what you actually believe and executing against the flow."
- "The biggest challenge for founders is to develop a sense of what you actually believe, then having a mechanism by which you're constantly learning from the ecosystem versus just digging your heels in."

**Why in guide:** Wang was 19 when he started Scale. The practical tactics — conviction from first principles, treating unsexy problems with rigor, irrational self-belief as strategy — translate directly to @Seventydotleven building against the grain in the Rust/AI infra space.

## What 'Do Things That Don't Scale' Really Means (Dalton Caldwell & Michael Seibel) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4RMjQal_c4U — transcribed — STRONG

**Thesis:** "Do things that don't scale" means you personally do low-status, manual work to get initial traction. The principle is recruit users manually first, then automate. Not: stay manual forever.

**Frameworks:**
- **Reddit:** Steve and Alexis invented fake usernames and submitted fake links themselves to make the site look alive. Day one: two people made it look like a real community. The moment when a real third-party submitted a link = major milestone.
- **DoorDash:** Founders delivered food themselves. Benefits: (1) learned why delivery was hard, (2) built restaurant owner relationships personally, (3) talked to users face-to-face ("here's your burrito, I'm the founder, do you like it?"). "Could you imagine a more low-status thing? That's why it was awesome."
- **Cruise:** First autonomous car was basically adaptive cruise control that couldn't handle shadows on highway 101. Kyle had a big red button on the floor. Google had fancy retrofitted equipment. Kyle had an Audi that couldn't deal with shadows. "That's how it starts."
- **The Job of Pre-PMF CEO:** "The job of the CEO is to do the shittiest, worst, low-status work you can think of. It's the opposite of whatever your mental model is when someone says CEO." Must be personally accountable for making the entire system run end-to-end. Smart people don't want to do unglamorous work — that's why they're not founders.
- **Embrace the customer pain directly:** You will learn 10x more than incumbents who outsource user research. Senior management of incumbents is not on the front line. You will build a better product because of what you learn by doing the work yourself.

**Quotes:**
- "You've got to learn to love and embrace the shitty work. That is what actually doing things that don't scale is."
- "Smart people don't tend to want to do unglamorous work. It's a special kind of person who is smart but will also get in their car and go pick up that order."

**Why in guide:** Concrete antidote to the performance of entrepreneurship. The examples of Reddit, DoorDash, and Cruise are the clearest illustrations of the do-things-that-don't-scale principle.

## Culture (Brian Chesky & Alfred Lin) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RfWgVWGEuGE — transcribed — STRONG

**Thesis:** Culture = core values × real actions × mission. It is a daily habit, not a mission statement. It's what people do when no one is watching.

**Frameworks:**
- **Definition:** "Every day, the core values and real actions of each member of the team in pursuit of the company's mission." Culture is the resolution to a bandwidth problem — the founder can't be in every decision, so culture encodes what you'd decide.
- **Why It Compounds:** Fortune 100 "best companies to work for" returned 11.08% annually (1997-2003) vs. S&P 500 (~6%). Strong culture companies roughly 2x market return.
- **Building Core Values:** Start with your own personal values. Add: what values do people you've never liked working with have? Their opposite should be yours. ## Section Body — The Guide's Own Wisdom for This Section

The guide's distilled principles for Founder Mind (from CLAUDE.md section):

### ▸ Ideas are noticed by a prepared mind, not generated in a brainstorm
The set of plausible-sounding ideas is huge; the set of good ones is tiny. They look identical from outside. Only someone who lived in a domain for years can tell them apart. Gates heard about the Altair like thousands of others, but he'd spent years on interpreters. "Live in the future, build what's missing." Deep domain residence in Rust-via-PyO3 and AI lead-gen is exactly the kind of preparation that lets you see what others can't.

### ▸ The schlep IS the business
Your unconscious filters out the best ideas before you consciously see them because the work looks tedious. Stripe: every developer knew payments were painful, nobody built it because bank deals + fraud + compliance got filtered pre-consideration. The bypass question: *what problem do I wish someone else would solve for me?*

### ▸ A real opportunity has three parts, and most people stop at two
Conventional wisdom + contrarian truth + tech enabler that makes the truth doable NOW. Everyone with a contrarian take stops at part two. The timing check is where theses die.

### ▸ The founder gene is resourcefulness, not determination or IQ
Graham's two-word definition: relentlessly resourceful. The opposite of hapless. Hapless is passivity in the face of a novel obstacle. Resourceful means you have your way with the world instead of the world having its way with you.

### ▸ Earnestness is the moat you can't fake at scale
Genuine care about the problem is both the strongest motivator and a real moat. Graham's formula: formidable + earnest = unstoppable. The 90% perform ambition. This is also the cheapest filter for cofounders.

### ▸ Inaction is a hidden form of risk
Altman's line: "inaction is a particularly insidious type of risk" because it feels safe. It's easier to do a hard thing that really matters than an easy thing that doesn't.

### ▸ Your discomfort with "manager-mode" advice is correct detection
Founder Mode names the gaslighting dynamic: founders feel wrong about being in the details and assume the feeling is insecurity. It's accurate signal. For a small team, don't prematurely install the manager-mode machinery.
