## How to Get Your First Users (YC Library) — https://www.ycombinator.com/library/ND-how-to-get-your-first-users — partial — STRONG

**Thesis:** The 0→100 user problem is a manual, not a marketing, problem. Build a minimum evolvable product — one that adapts to early users — and go find them by hand, not by funnel.

**Key frameworks:**
- **Minimum Evolvable Product (MEP):** Not MVP as in "bare minimum to ship" — MEP as in "designed to mutate based on the first cohort's feedback." The first 100 users are collaborators who define the product's actual shape.
- **Concierge first:** Do the thing manually before automating it. The manual version teaches you what to automate; the automated version teaches you nothing.
- **The cold-start radius:** Your first users exist in concentric rings — (1) direct network, (2) their network, (3) relevant online communities, (4) strangers with the problem. Work inward-to-outward, not outward-to-inward.

**Tactics (verified from YC context and Gustav Alstromer recap):**
- **Direct outreach to strangers with the problem** — not blasting, but identifying 20-30 specific people who obviously have the pain and writing them individually. Response rate is high when the message names their actual problem.
- **Community seeding** — find the Reddit/Slack/Discord/forum where your users already talk, spend time there as a real member first, then share genuinely. Magic got 40,000 signups overnight from a single Reddit/HN share.
- **Show HN and Product Hunt** as repeatable launch channels (not once-ever events); YC data shows they now convert comparably to a TechCrunch launch.
- **Startup School forum** (40K founders) and **Bookface** (4K YC founders) as low-risk first-launch surfaces.
- **Friends and family as test-not-rely:** Share with them to validate your pitch, not to validate demand — friends are a bad proxy for real users.
- **Lugg example (strangers):** Founders literally rented trucks, stood outside IKEA, approached people who'd bought large furniture. Confirmed hair-on-fire demand before writing a line of code.

**Why it's in the guide:** Gives permission to be completely manual and non-scalable with the first 100 users. "Doing things that don't scale" is not a phase you survive — it's the foundation that determines your word-of-mouth ceiling.

## The Best Way to Launch Your Startup (YC / Kat Mañalac) — https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ir-the-best-way-to-launch-your-startup — read — STRONG

**Thesis:** "I want to destroy the idea that launching is just one moment in time." Most startups treat launch as a single high-stakes event; the correct model is 10–20 micro-launches throughout the company's lifetime, each targeting a different audience or angle.

**Key frameworks:**
- **Launch types (sequential, not one-and-done):**
  1. **Silent launch** — domain, one-line pitch, CTA (email signup). Does nothing for growth; gets you live and iterable. Minimum: you own your URL and can be linked.
  2. **Friends & family** — validate pitch clarity, not demand. Don't linger; they're not your users.
  3. **Strangers with the problem** — the first real launch. Communities, forums, direct outreach.
  4. **Online communities** — Reddit, HN, Slack, Discord. Write conversationally, no jargon, invite discussion ("I built X because I had problem Y — anyone else face this?"). Share surprising insight or intellectual curiosity angle.
  5. **Request for access / waitlist** — build viral mechanics in. Superhuman: referral bonuses skip queue; email signature generates inbound referral requests.
  6. **Press and blogger outreach** — Joy (wedding site builder) reached 50+ bloggers; 4 responded; drove significant early growth. Skip pay-to-play sponsorships completely.
  7. **Pre-orders** — Sheertex: compelling video → pitch to press → HN launch → Product Hunt → social amplification. Sequence matters.
  8. **New feature launches** — each feature is a re-launch opportunity. Stripe: community engagement + blog + social + press, coordinated. Glossier: every 6-8 weeks, all channels simultaneously.
  9. **Build your own community** — accumulate an email list before you need it. Gadi Evron: built list pre-launch, sent to them when TechCrunch published, investors amplified it.

**Specific tactics:**
- Show HN framing: write as a builder sharing a thing, not as a marketer pitching a product. "Ask HN: I built X to solve Y" outperforms "Launch: X is the best way to Z."
- Join communities before you need them. If you're new, get an intro from an existing member.
- Track micro-metrics per launch channel: signups/channel, not total signups. Know which channel your real users come from.

**Memorable quote:** "I don't want you to spend all of Startup School getting ready for this one big launch day."

**Numbers:** Reddit's founders started with their 8 YC batchmates as first users. Magic: 40,000 overnight from one community share.

**Why it's in the guide:** Kills the "one big launch" mythology. The founders who get good distribution launch constantly — every new feature, every blog post, every Show HN is another cast of the line.

## How to Find & Pull Startup Growth Levers — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xelqH9n12zw — transcribed — STRONG
*Matt Lerner · First Round · 66 min*

**Thesis:** 95% of PayPal's growth came from 5 things. The key job in early-stage is finding your 10% of work that drives 90% of results before running out of money — and that requires starting with customer psychology, not channels.

**Key frameworks:**

**1. The Locksmith Moment**
When doing customer research, look for the moment the customer suddenly and urgently realizes they need your product — "I'm standing right here looking at this gate." That's your acquisition context. Where are your customers when they most acutely feel the pain? Turn up there, in that form.

**2. Map the growth model (behavioral metrics funnel)**
Before doing anything else: map every step from "first becomes aware" to "happy engaged user" with rough conversion numbers. Don't get deep into analytics — just pencil-sketch numbers. The map reveals your actual bottleneck. Wrong: working on brand; correct: working on whatever step is losing the most people.

**3. Theory of constraints applied to growth**
A refinery makes money on gallons per hour. The bottleneck determines throughput. Improve the bottleneck → whole system speeds up. Improve anything else → waste. "Unfortunately a startup's a little more complicated than a refinery that literally has pipes and liquid flowing through it. But still you can look at each stage."

**4. Jobs-to-be-done customer interviews (the pre-channel work)**
- Interview recent signups while purchase is fresh. (Non-buyers will mislead you — they may not be your actual customers.)
- Q1: "Tell me in your own words what you bought." (Anchors to purchase)
- Q2: "What will that enable you to achieve?" (Gets to the goal, not the product)
- Keep drilling past functional goals to emotional/social goals.
- Look for: the single goal your best customers all share. That one outcome becomes your entire positioning.
- "Photo books in 5 minutes" → quadrupled install conversion overnight. "Fast, easy photo books" meant nothing; "5 minutes" meant everything. The difference was one customer interview.

**5. The Northstar metric (pre-channel)**
Must measure value delivered to customers, not revenue. Weekly actives, daily actives, photo books made, memories solidified — something that directly tracks "is the customer getting the thing they hired us for?"

**6. Founder-led growth mandate**
- "You never hire someone else to be your head of product before you make your first product. Why do you think you're going to hire someone up the street who can do growth?"
- Founders who outsource early growth are paying someone to not have the context required to find the lever.
- First growth hires: junior bright generalists. Former scientists with analytical bias + people skills. Not "growth hackers."
- Growth Sprints: 1-2 week cycles. Document: observation → hypothesis → prediction → outcome metric → result → why-weird-thing-happened. This eliminates hindsight bias.

**7. The three failure modes**
- **Overthinkers** — debate, theorize, never execute
- **Underthinkers** — build-build-build without understanding why nothing sticks; add features until codebase is unmaintainable
- **Hire-and-delegate** — bring in channel experts who lack company context, founders disengage from the mechanism

**8. The "you already have the answer" trap**
"A lot of times when you find [the growth lever] it looks distasteful. If you've been doing this for two years you probably tried this idea and for some reason it didn't make sense." The reason you're not growing is missing information, not missing effort. When you find the missing piece, it will often feel too simple or not impressive enough.

**Tactics + Numbers:**
- Popsa (photo books): Ranking #1 in App Store for "photo books" with low install rate. Changed "fast easy photo books" to "photo books in 5 minutes" → 4x install conversion overnight → $40M revenue run rate, profitable, 5M photo books sold.
- FATMAP (3D terrain maps): Had database of 300K trail maps but pages loaded slow. Simple fix — load static image first, map in background. Traffic spiked, company acquired.
- SonicJobs: Low activation. Changed welcome email from generic categories to 15 specific job titles in their users' exact language → doubled activation rate from one email change.

**Memorable quote:** "Standing between a startup and success is some missing information... strategizing and executing are great ways to execute but very slow ways to learn."

**Why it's in the guide:** Pre-channel customer psychology work is the thing most technically-minded founders skip. Lerner's framework makes it concrete: interview recent signups, find the locksmith moment, position to that, THEN pick a channel.

## Growth (Alex Schultz, Facebook VP Growth) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vJqlG5ytLDs — transcribed — MUST
*YC Startup School · 47 min*

**Thesis:** Retention is the single most important thing for growth. No growth tactic, virality hack, or channel investment saves bad retention. Fix retention first; growth is the multiplier on top.

**Key frameworks:**

**1. The retention curve test (the only PMF signal that matters)**
Plot: % of users who are monthly active on their Nth day after registration, for all cohorts. Does the curve asymptote to a line parallel to the x-axis? If yes → viable business, product-market fit for some segment. If the curve declines to zero → broken. "If it doesn't flatten out, don't go and do growth tactics. Don't hire a growth hacker. Focus on getting product-market fit."
- Facebook used this for B2B advertisers when the product was 3 days old. Within 90 days predicted one-year advertiser value to 97% accuracy.
- You can do this with as few as 10,000 customers.

**2. What "good retention" looks like (dimensional reasoning)**
Don't ask Schultz "what's the benchmark?" — figure it out yourself via dimensional reasoning. Facebook: ~1.3B MAU out of ~2B internet users (at that time). WhatsApp: 600M active / number of smartphones. Amazon: essentially 100% of US online shoppers have accounts. "Different verticals need different terminal retention rates. Social media: if your first batch isn't ~80% retained, you're not going to have a massive social network. E-commerce: 20-30% monthly retention is probably fine."

**3. The Northstar metric — owned by the CEO**
- Every company needs a different northstar. Mark Zuckerberg: Monthly active users (not registered users). Yan (WhatsApp): Number of sends (not users). Airbnb: Nights booked (not revenue). eBay: Gross merchandise volume (not revenue).
- When Schultz joined Facebook: changed affiliate program from paying for Confirmed Registered Users → Activated CRUs (had to bid/buy/list after confirming). Overnight: CRU dropped 20%, ACRU dropped only 5%, ratio improved dramatically, ACRU growth accelerated massively. Why: affiliates now landed people on the trampoline search page, not the registration page — users found the thing they wanted, got excited, then registered.
- "If you want to drive CRUs, you land them on the registration page. If you want to drive ACRUs, you land them on the search results page so they see the thing they want."

**4. The magic moment — the single most important product metric**
Find the moment when a new user first experiences the core value that makes them stick. Design everything to get new users there as fast as possible.
- Facebook: "See your friends." The first photo of a friend = the magic moment. The metric: 10 friends in 14 days.
- Airbnb: Finding that first listing; being paid as a host for the first time.
- eBay: Finding that Pez dispenser you'd been looking for.
- Rule: Move up where the retention curve has already asymptoted. Then you go from 50% → 60% → 70% retention.

**5. Optimize for the marginal user, not yourself**
"Every single company when they talk about notifications goes, 'I'm getting too many notifications.' Are your power users leaving because of that? No. Focus on the marginal user."
- Facebook resurrected users had low friend counts. Biggest lever: get them to 10 friends. Not notifications, not features — connections.
- Facebook growth accounting: New users + Resurrected users - Churned users = Net growth. Resurrection and churn dominate new users once you reach any scale.

**6. Virality framework (Shawn Parker's model)**
Three components: Payload × Frequency × Conversion Rate = viral coefficient K.
- **Payload:** How many people can you reach per blast? (Hotmail: low — one email at a time. Facebook email invite: medium.)
- **Frequency:** How often does the message hit the same person? (Hotmail: very high — every email.)
- **Conversion rate:** % who sign up after seeing the invitation. (PayPal seller side: nearly 100% — "I will send you money via this" is a near-perfect conversion.)
- Hotmail: low payload + very high frequency + high conversion = massively viral.
- PayPal: low payload + low frequency + near-100% conversion on seller side + cash incentive on consumer side = viral.
- Facebook: NOT viral via email mechanics. Grew via pure word-of-mouth because it was genuinely good.
- K-factor example: 100 people hit per import × 10% click × 50% signup × 15% subsequently import = K=0.75, not viral. Getting K above 1 requires improving one or more of these legs.

**7. Internationalization: Facebook's biggest mistake**
"Facebook internationalized too late. Cheryl said it broadly in public and I definitely agree." Result: clones (Studio Vet with `fakebook.css` in their HTML, Contactia, Mixie, Orkut) captured markets while Facebook was US-focused.
- Fix: Moved slow to move fast. Wrapped all strings in FBT, built community translation platform. French translated in 12 hours. Reached 104 languages, 70 of which community-translated. Priority: FIGS (French, Italian, German, Spanish) — though by his talk's time, Hindi was the fastest-growing language (quadrupled in one year).

**8. SEO framework (three legs)**
1. **Keyword research**: Supply (how competitive), demand (search volume), value (to your business). Tools: Google Adwords Keyword Planner. Schultz spent a year optimizing for "cocktail making" — 500 searches/month in UK. Should have optimized for "cocktail recipes" or "drink recipes" (US).
2. **Links**: Page rank still the primary driver. One change at Facebook — added a directory so Google could spider to all public user profiles — 100x'd SEO traffic.
3. **Table stakes**: XML sitemaps, correct headers, internal link distribution.

**9. Email/SMS/Push: the three rules**
1. **Deliverability first** — shared servers, dirty IPs, and spam behavior will get you blacklisted. Hard bounces: retry once or twice then stop.
2. **Notifications > newsletters** — "Don't do newsletters. Someone who signed up yesterday versus someone who's been on your product 3 years — do they need the same message? No."
3. **Trigger-based > batch** — Facebook's first-like notification for low-engaged users (not power users) dramatically increased click-through. eBay's "first cross-border transaction" email: highest CTR they ever ran.
4. Email is essentially dead for users under 25; use WhatsApp, SMS, push instead.

**On execution velocity:** "If you can run more experiments than the next guy, if you can be hungry for growth, if you fight and die for every extra user and you stay up late at night to get those extra users... you will grow faster. Mark said he thinks we won because we wanted it more."

**Memorable quotes:**
- "Retention is the single most important thing for growth."
- "Don't hire a growth hacker. Focus on getting product-market fit."
- "A good plan violently executed today is better than a perfect plan tomorrow." (Patton)

**Why it's in the guide:** The canonical lecture. Retention-first is not a cliché — Schultz operationalizes it with actual curve math and eBay/Facebook case studies. The northstar + magic moment + marginal-user framework gives a complete operating model for early-stage growth.

## The New AI Growth Playbook 2026: Lovable to $200M (Lenny/Elena Verna) — https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-new-ai-growth-playbook-for-2026-elena-verna — read — MUST

**Thesis:** 60-70% of conventional growth tactics break on AI products. The reason: traditional growth assumed stable product-market fit. AI products operate on a quarterly PMF treadmill — the competitive landscape shifts faster than any optimization cycle. The new playbook inverts SaaS orthodoxy: ship over optimize, product team owns activation, free tier is the primary acquisition channel.

**Key frameworks:**

**1. The Product-Market Fit Treadmill**
Every AI company must re-validate PMF every 3 months. Competitive moats evaporate within quarters. Feature differentiation becomes commoditized with each model release. Operational implication: product roadmap shifts from annual → quarterly cycles. Cohort analysis must track whether new cohorts experience the same "magic" as early ones. If new cohorts are delighting less → PMF has degraded, not just retention.

**2. Innovation-over-optimization mandate**
Traditional SaaS: Amplitude-style metrics optimization → conversion improvement → scale. AI: building capabilities competitors cannot replicate matters more than marginal conversion improvements. "Shipping features over optimization work" is the strategic principle, not a tradeoff.

**3. Product team owns activation (not growth team)**
In SaaS, growth teams owned onboarding funnels. In AI products, the core product experience must be immediately magical — poor initial experience cannot be salvaged by A/B testing the signup flow. This means product teams bear responsibility for first-use delight; growth teams shift to discovery, awareness, and adjacent-use-case identification.

**4. The "Minimum Lovable Product" standard**
Not MVP. Minimum lovable product: demonstrates capability immediately. In AI markets with dozens of alternatives, poor first impressions generate permanent skepticism rather than retry behavior. "Initial product impressions determine platform adoption."

**5. Free tier as the primary growth engine (not a funnel)**
Lovable's counterintuitive finding: free tier usage generates more growth than paid advertising. This differs from traditional freemium because the free product IS the distribution mechanism — users share remarkable things they built, which drives acquisition at near-zero marginal cost.

**6. Growth mechanics at $200M ARR in one year (Lovable specific)**
- **Weekly feature velocity**: Continuous capability shipping maintains competitive differentiation and gives growth teams new acquisition angles weekly.
- **Missions/challenges**: Built into the product to naturally encourage sharing.
- **Template + starting-point libraries**: Lower friction to first value; give new users a running start.
- **Integration partnerships**: Shopify + Lovable created a distribution channel that bypassed traditional paid acquisition entirely.
- **Community activation**: SheBuilds (women builders initiative) created cultural differentiation beyond product features.

**7. Why paid advertising underperforms**
The product tells the story better than ads. Word-of-mouth from users creating novel applications spreads faster than any creative. "Brand awareness builds through impressive outputs, not messaging."

**8. Practical PMF re-validation signals**
- Are new cohorts experiencing equivalent delight to early cohorts?
- Is the "magic moment" still happening at same rate?
- Are engagement curves for month-3 cohorts similar to month-1 cohorts from 6 months ago?
- If curves are flattening at a lower level → PMF degradation, requires product innovation not growth optimization.

**Reference companies:** Lovable ($200M ARR in <1 year, ~100 people), Cursor ($300M ARR), Gamma ($100M ARR), Captions — all operating under the innovation-over-optimization doctrine.

**Why it's in the guide:** Live from inside the fastest ARR ramp in SaaS history. Verna is the most credible source on what actually works for AI-native products in 2025-2026, as opposed to what worked for SaaS in 2018.

## How to Consistently Go Viral: Nikita Bier's Playbook — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bhnfZhJWCWY — transcribed — MUST
*Lenny's Podcast · 1h38m*

**Thesis:** Viral growth is a science, not luck. With certainty, if you're good at your job, you can make an app grow and go viral. The key is: (1) find latent demand where people are going through a distorted process to get a value — crystallize what they're trying to do, (2) make acquisition the primary action designed from day one, not a feature bolted on later, (3) validate in sequence (core flow → spreads in peer group → hops peer groups → monetizes), and (4) always design positive-sum viral mechanics.

**Background / credibility:**
- TBH: 40% of the target school downloaded in first 24 hours, #1 in US in 6 days, 360,000 installs/day at peak. Sold to Facebook for $30M+, 9 weeks after launch.
- Gas: rebuilt viral mechanics from scratch (regulatory environment changed in 5 years), 10M downloads, $11M in sales, sold to Discord.
- Advisor to 35-36 companies including Dupe, Saturn (now #1 in productivity App Store), BeReal, Citizen, Flow, Wealthsimple.
- Built 15+ consumer apps before TBH; first mobile app took 1 year, last took 2 weeks.

**Key frameworks:**

**1. Latent demand detection**
"The way you should search for product ideas is this concept of a latent demand — where people are trying to obtain a particular value and going through a very distortive process to obtain that value. If you can crystallize what their motivation is, you can have this kind of intense adoption."
- TBH origin: Saw teens playing a manual TBH game on Snapchat. Found the #1 app in the US was an Arabic-language anonymous messaging app (Sarahah). "The entire app was in Arabic. Like, the strongest signal that you could ever have that people want something."
- Five Labs (before TBH): Used Cambridge Analytica's exact personality model on public Facebook posts → tens of millions of profiles in 3 days.

**2. Why teens (and why adults don't grow social apps organically)**
"For every social app I've ever built, the number of invitations sent per user drops 20% for every additional year of age from 13 to 18." If you build for adults, expect to acquire every user with ads. Adults over 22 essentially stop adopting new communication products. Their social graph stops growing at ~21. "Most likely you'll never get network effects" with adult users.
- Academic finding he cites: Number of people you text peaks at ~21, then collapses. Rises again only in retirement. If you need network effects from a communication tool, target the upward part of the curve.

**3. Reproducible testing process**
"The thing that will actually influence the probability of your success more than anything else is having a reproducible testing process — taking many shots at bat." 
- How Bier seeded test apps into schools: created dedicated Instagram accounts for each school, followed everyone whose bio showed the school name, accepted follow-backs. "This is how we tested apps. This is NOT how we grew the app." Used to validate whether the product had legs — eliminated confounding variables (did they have enough friends? did they reach the aha moment?).
- Live chat customer support 24/7 in app: "I know it sounds insane, but users will literally tell you what the problem they're having. That eliminates another confounding variable." Used to get signal and ensure users feel taken care of, which improves organic word-of-mouth.

**4. Sequential validation — the conditional hypothesis chain**
"If this is true, then what next needs to be true for this to work? Four things that must be true for the thing to work."
- Gas rebuild phases: (1) Will people use the core flow? (2) Will it spread within a peer group? (3) Will it hop peer groups? (4) Will people pay for it?
- "Execute at 100% for the thing you're trying to validate at this specific stage, and you can half-ass the rest. Don't try to validate everything at once."

**5. Design positive-sum viral mechanics (the ethics constraint)**
"The internet is a living, breathing thing. If you do the wrong thing by users, the internet will come back and defend itself." Every product Bier built used only ethical, above-board mechanics. Anonymous polling → always positive messages (polls only generate compliments, never negatives). Gas received daily messages from users saying the app helped them reconsider self-harm.
- Name matters for viral spread: "Crush" → 35/65 male/female split, men wouldn't invite friends to app with pink icon called "Crush." Changed to "Gas," black flame icon → invite rate jumped immediately.

**6. Three reasons people download apps**
Bier's distillation from 10+ years: (1) Make or save money (WhatsApp: free texting). (2) Find a mate (Tinder, Snapchat). (3) Unplug from reality (Netflix, Fortnite). Sub-utilitarian category: movement/shelter (Uber, Airbnb). Putting this framing into a pitch document and being honest about which one your app serves is essential — hard to defend in a large company meeting, essential for building the right viral mechanic.

**7. Time-to-value = 3 seconds**
"In 2024, people's attention spans are like 3 seconds. If you can't demonstrate value in the first 3 seconds, it's over." Dupe case study: Bier identified the DealHop feature (paste a URL, find cheapest version online). Needed a memorable, iconic delivery mechanism. Bought dupe.com — users just prepend the domain to any product URL. "One tap to expose a ton of value." Under 60 days from launch: millions in ARR.

**8. iOS contact permission changes (2024) — the seismic shift**
iOS 18's new granular contact permissions effectively killed contact-sync-based growth loops. Old flow: 65% approval, entire contact list synced, system finds friends and ranks by likely app adopters. New flow: granular alphabetical picker, most users select 0-3 contacts, friend discovery collapses. "If you're betting on contact sync right now, you better start thinking about Plan B. Most apps will not have social graphs going forward — this will entrench incumbents even more."

**9. The marketing-product unity principle**
"A lot of founders separate marketing and product growth — top-of-funnel growth from the product's growth mechanisms. They're both the same." When targeting a specific community, the ads must show imagery of that community, the in-app experience must let them join that community, and the invite flow must mention that community. All must be aligned for the acquisition flywheel to spin. "A lot of people really screw that up."

**10. When you have a PMF signal**
"If there's any uncertainty, it's not working. When a consumer product is working, you'll know." Bier's tell: the metric becomes hourly actives per day, not daily actives. "If your product's working, you'll know" (Roger Dickey). Everything breaks every 3 days because you're growing too fast to keep up. Geofencing a viral app to control growth pace is often the right call.

**11. Large companies can't build zero-to-one**
Facebook/Meta PM culture: mostly "writing documents and being the team secretary." Designers run products at Snap and Apple — better for innovation. Large companies respond to competitive threats in 12-24 months (framing deck → VP review → development). Incentive structures require market evidence before pitching; pure first-principles ideas can't be defended politically.

**Memorable quotes:**
- "With certainty, if you're good at your job, you can make an app grow and go viral."
- "The strongest signal that you could ever have that people want something." (on Sarahah being #1 in US while entirely in Arabic)
- "Products live and die in the pixels."
- "The key thing you have to know when you have a hoax spreading about your app is you really have to make sure the hoax is less viral than your app." (on Gas's human trafficking hoax crisis)

**Why it's in the guide:** The most concentrated tactical knowledge about consumer viral mechanics from the person with the best empirical track record. TBH and Gas are not luck — they're the output of a testable, repeatable process. The latent-demand detection method and sequential conditional validation are directly applicable to any B2C product.

## How to Build Networks, Movements, AI-Native Products (Chris Dixon, a16z) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tJdtt8n0Kgw — transcribed — OPTIONAL
*a16z consumer pod · 43 min*

**Thesis:** The three exponential forces in tech (Moore's Law, composability, network effects) overwhelm any tactical product decisions. Find which side of these forces you're on before optimizing anything else. For AI products: most are currently in the skeuomorphic phase, imitating prior forms; the native AI phase (genuinely new interaction models) hasn't fully arrived yet. Build tools first, and plan deliberately for when and how to layer a network.

**Key frameworks:**

**1. Come for the tool, stay for the network (revisited)**
Instagram's early filters were the tool: gave away premium photo effects for free. Network value came second. Instagram piggybacked on Twitter to bootstrap distribution before its own graph had value. Substack piggybacked on email + Twitter. Stripe Link piggybacked on merchant distribution to build a consumer payment network. Shopify built merchant tools first, then layered Shop (consumer network) on top.
- Key insight: "Network effects cut both ways. They're great when you have them but very hard in the beginning." The tool is what makes it worth using before the network exists. But single-player tools are hard to defend — competitors can copy features.

**2. Externalized network effects**
New thesis for AI era: "Maybe a lot of the network effect has been externalized to the internet." Cursor has no in-product network, but has thousands of YouTube tutorials, influencers, how-to guides, and SEO presence. The surrounding ecosystem IS the network effect. "Being first to own the meme in a category and getting that effect going" is the timing advantage that matters — then product velocity and quality maintain it.

**3. Finding movements (the niche community pattern)**
Look for "hyperenthusiastic, sometimes cultish" niche communities with their own language, norms, and insider/outsider sense. These are leading indicators:
- 20K hardcore enthusiasts often become the creation and marketing engine for a product.
- Pattern: Bitcoin (small Reddit community) → Coinbase. Oculus (Palmer Lucky Kickstarter VR enthusiasts) → $2B Facebook acquisition. Makerbot (3D printing hobbyists). Soylent (nootropics/quantified self community).
- Test: "The things you learn more about and they become more interesting — that's the signal. Things that seem silly when you learn more — discard."
- Underlying requirement: must have exponential forces behind it. 3D printing never became a trillion-dollar category because it has linear forces, not exponential. Neutropics → same.
- Current movement with exponential forces behind it: vibe coding ("an irreversible consumer phenomenon where everybody is creating software").

**4. Skeuomorphic vs. native phase**
Every new platform goes through: (1) Imitate prior media form (early film looked like plays; early internet looked like print brochures/catalogs), (2) Discover native grammar (YouTube → user-generated video; social networking → real identity connections).
- AI is currently mostly skeuomorphic — using AI to do what illustrators/writers/assistants already did, but faster.
- The native phase: interaction models that couldn't have existed before AI. The entrepreneurs who figure out what that looks like will dominate.
- "I personally think we're in a skeuomorphic phase right now. The native phase is going to be crazier and more interesting."

**5. The idea maze**
Original concept from Balaji Srinivasan. The maze you enter (health AI, image generation, coding assistant) matters — but so does your agility within it. Netflix: started with mail-order DVDs, pivoted to streaming, pivoted to original content. The core maze (internet → subscription movies) was right; the implementation pivoted twice. "You need to think: am I willing to be in this maze for 10 years? Am I willing to persevere through emotionally challenging periods?"

**6. On moats in AI**
- Network effects: hard to build natively in most AI products today.
- Brand: underappreciated. ChatGPT is a household name almost overnight — brand effects are more powerful than SV typically acknowledges.
- Capital: "Do well → raise the most money → capital becomes a moat." AI already shows this barbelling.
- Domain depth: "Go so deep on a domain that no matter what the incumbent models do, you'll always have an edge in your product."
- High-DAU products: possibly sufficient if product velocity maintains the cutting edge (expensive in AI due to model costs).

**Quote:** "There are no marketing problems, only product problems — because the technology allows you to be so ambitious on behalf of your customer."

**Why it's in the guide:** Dixon provides the investor-level framing for thinking about structural forces vs. tactics. Useful for deciding what kind of product to build (tool vs. network, movement-aware vs. not) — less useful for execution-level growth tactics.

## Unorthodox Frameworks for Growth: Adjacent Users — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ub9UkWByFIQ — transcribed — STRONG
*Bangaly Kaba (ex-Instagram Head of Growth) · Lenny's · 1h42m*

**Thesis:** When your retention curves start declining — not because the product broke, but because the product isn't right for the next cohort coming through the door — you're meeting your adjacent user. Understanding who they are and rebuilding for them is the mechanism that took Instagram from 40M to 200M+ users and is the most important, most-ignored growth lever for any product growing past its initial core.

**Background / credibility:** Early growth PM at Facebook (people recommendations, friend connections), Head of Growth at Instagram (40M → 1B+ users), VP Product at Instacart, Director PM at YouTube. Framework has been independently applied by TikTok's growth team.

**Key frameworks:**

**1. Adjacent user theory — the core model**
Your "adjacent user" is the person who could be using your product but for some reason it doesn't fully work for them. They're not your core user (who already loves it) and not a random person (who has no interest) — they're one step outside the current circle of people the product actually serves.

Detection signal: Cohort retention curves declining not because of product bugs, but because new cohorts (who are inherently different from early adopters) are getting less value from the same product. "We'd see people sign up and then leave after 7-8-9 months. We also saw a later dip, which was very weird."

Instagram case study (2016): Women in the US in their 30s would say "why would I have Instagram? I have Facebook." Then a year later: "of course I use Instagram." The product hadn't changed as much as the mental model of who Instagram was for.

**2. The connections pivot — Instagram's biggest lever**
Problem: Instagram was recommending celebrities to everyone (all follows equal in the algorithm). Result: new users followed 50+ celebrities, made their first post, and received 0 engagement because none of their friends followed them back. This caused a second retention dip at months 7-9.
Fix: The connections pivot (2017) — prioritize connecting new users to real friends over celebrities. Made the case to Kevin/Mike that this was necessary despite the clear short-term cost to celebrity account growth.
Result: "Our retention literally doubled over the course of about a year and a half." Combined with Stories and top-of-funnel work, drove the 40-50% YoY growth periods visible in public records.

**3. Instagram's multi-layer growth stack (revealed)**
Most people think Instagram grew via virality + word of mouth. The actual stack:
1. **Invitations** — core viral loop, friend invites
2. **Celebrity partnerships** — team was in place before Bangaly joined. Taught celebrities how to tell their story on Instagram. This set the norms for how the platform gets used.
3. **SEO** — launched web presence (huge fight internally, Kevin/Mike didn't believe in it initially). Celebrities + news media linking to Instagram profiles → massive inbound link graph → Instagram shows up top 5 for nearly every person's Google search today. "Launching web increased Instagram's growth by 10% the minute we launched."
4. **Embeds** — Instagram embeds in news articles created both SEO value and brand awareness.
5. **Paid media** — layered on top of the above signals.
All five compounding: "Every time invitations got better, every time we got more celebrities, every time SEO got better — it magnified the top of funnel."

**4. Account access churn — the most impactful single experiment**
Problem: Hundreds of thousands of people per day could not get back into their Instagram accounts. After 28 days of failed logins, they never returned. Losing 10-12 million users per year to account access issues.
Root cause: Multiple login methods (email, handle, phone) each on separate tabs. No graceful recovery flow.
Fix: 
1. Omnibox login (put email/phone/handle in one box)
2. If fail twice on trusted device → automatic text verification
3. On logout: offer to save credentials + later added PIN option
Result: Solved ~half the problem each. Recovered ~15-20M monthly active users per year. Unexpected finding: people were getting locked out of their second and third accounts too, creating massive unexplored content creation. Led to creation of the multi-accounts team (still exists as a major product surface today).

**5. TikTok's application of adjacent user theory**
Alex Zhu (TikTok growth): "Instagram is like Europe — if you're King of Europe you never want to go to America." TikTok's problem: Instagram celebrities had no incentive to come to TikTok. Solution: don't target Instagram's celebrities — target the people who want to be king but can't win on Instagram. "Make your own celebrities." TikTok went after the B and C-list creators who were frustrated by their ceiling on Instagram. "He used the adjacent user theory on us." Adjacent to Instagram's power users → the aspirational creator class.

**6. The "understand work" principle**
Anti-pattern: "identify → justify → execute" (someone has a good idea, pulls data to justify it, builds it). Better: "understand → identify → execute." Start from first principles what's actually going on.
- Instacart example: People were coming in to buy groceries, but 90% of orders after 5 purchases are the same items. The product didn't make reordering easy (7-8 clicks, couldn't take partial orders). Fix: streamlined reorder flow. "That was probably one of the biggest growth wins in Instacart's history."
- Dog-fooding in adjacent user state: "Use the product as if you were a new user in a specific job-to-be-done context, not as a power user." Power user experience is not representative of what new users see. "If you go create a new Gmail account and look at YouTube today, I guarantee it's a significantly worse experience."

**7. The impact = environment × skills framework (career)**
Impact is the primary variable to optimize, not compensation or title. Environment variables: manager (most important), resources, scope, team quality, compensation, company culture. Score each 0-2. Low score = opportunity or reason to leave. "Manager is the most important variable — they have the ability to move the chess pieces around and fix many of the other variables."

**Memorable quotes:**
- "The world changed so fast — when I joined [Instagram at 445M MAU] women in their 30s would say 'why would I have Instagram?' A year later: 'of course I use Instagram.'"
- "People don't leave a job, they leave a manager."
- TikTok applying adjacent users: "He used the adjacent user theory on us."

**Why it's in the guide:** The adjacent user framework is the growth lever that's most commonly missed at Series A-B stage. It's not about better acquisition — it's about noticing when the product stops working for the next ring of users and rebuilding for them before churn signals it. Bangaly operationalizes it with concrete examples from Instagram's most important growth years.

## Section Body — The Guide's Own Wisdom for This Section

*From `BAD_GUIDE.md` — Distribution & Growth (B2C + launch-in-hours)*

### ▸ Pick one channel and go deep — most winners used exactly one.
Lenny Rachitsky synthesized 40+ consumer launches: almost every company got first 1,000 users through a single strategy. Spreading across five channels = zero on all five. Pick X-native virality, a specific community, or building-in-public — then 90% of effort there.

### ▸ Solve the cold-start problem before you scale anything.
Andrew Chen: don't need "users," need one atomic network — the smallest group for which the product is already useful. Get that atomic network to tip, then copy the motion. Applying escape-velocity tactics to a product that hasn't tipped its first network is the classic wrong-stage mistake.

### ▸ Build loops, not funnels.
Balfour/Winters: funnels leak, loops compound. The output of one cycle (a user, content, a referral) becomes the input to the next. A loop gives declining CAC over time; a funnel gives a flat-to-rising CAC treadmill.

### ▸ Design for bragging rights, not just utility.
Kevin Kwok on Superhuman: users tweeted because it made them look good (fast, exclusive, high-taste). Social capital drives the acquisition loop. Build the share moment around making the user look sharp to their audience.

### ▸ Come for the tool, stay for the network.
Chris Dixon sequencing rule: single-player utility first (Instagram's filters, Delicious's bookmarks), network value second. The tool gets you to critical mass before network effects can kick in. Make the product fully worth using for one person with zero other users.

### ▸ Retention sets your viral ceiling, not viral tricks.
Andrew Chen 2025 reframe: a product with K=0.25 and 18-month retention crushes one with K=0.8 and 30-day churn. Fix retention before touching the viral coefficient.

### ▸ Reverse-engineer where your users already congregate.
The "where do my users already congregate" mental model. Don't build an audience from scratch — plug into one that exists. People who'd pay for your product are posting publicly right now. That's a gift; instrument it.

### ▸ First 1,000 users: what actually worked (verified cases)
Cursor, Perplexity, Midjourney, ElevenLabs, v0, Bolt.new, Lovable — three moves did the work:
1. **Frictionless entry**: Cursor (free VS Code fork, no credit card), Perplexity (no login), Bolt.new (no signup — open URL, type, get running app) matched StackBlitz's 7 years of ARR in 48 hours off one tweet.
2. **Existing audience**: Cursor/v0 used developer ecosystems; v0's waitlist hit 100K in 3 weeks on Vercel's brand; Lovable converted a 52K-star open-source following (gpt-engineer) into #1 Product Hunt and #1 HN launch day.
3. **Viral demo moment, not PR**: ElevenLabs via meme voice clips; Midjourney via shared Discord gallery; Bolt via live-build X/Reddit/TikTok clips.

## → Top Actionable Takeaways for @Seventydotleven

**1. Launch your next thing 5+ times, not once.**
Kat Mañalac's framework: silent page today, friends/family this week, online communities next week, Show HN the week after. Each is a separate targeted cast. Pick the X founder/builder community as the primary community — you already live there. The product should be launchable in multiple configurations: as a tool, as a specific use case, as a "look what I found" demo moment.

**2. Do the Schultz retention curve test before spending a dollar on distribution.**
Plot % monthly active by day-from-registration for your first cohort. Does it asymptote or go to zero? If it goes to zero, you do not have PMF — no amount of distribution fixes this. Do this with 500-1,000 users. You will know within 90 days.

**3. Find your magic moment and drive every new user to it as fast as possible.**
What is the moment where a new user says "aha, this is what this is"? Design the entire onboarding and first-session experience to reach that moment in the first 3 minutes. Everything before that moment is friction to eliminate.

**4. Interview 10 recent signups before building your next acquisition funnel.**
Use Lerner's jobs-to-be-done protocol: "What did you buy?" → "What will that enable you to achieve?" → drill to real goal. Find the locksmith moment — where were they when they suddenly needed the product most? That context IS your acquisition channel. The one output sentence they all agree on is your whole positioning.

**5. Build the viral loop into the product's core action, not as a share button.**
Bier's lesson: TBH went viral because receiving a positive poll message was the thing that made you want to share. The viral mechanic WAS the product experience. If your product has to ask users to share, you haven't found the loop yet. What is the moment in your product where the user's natural next instinct is to tell someone?

**6. Validate in sequence: core flow → spreads in peer group → hops peer groups → monetizes.**
For any new product or feature: commit 100% effort to validating one stage before moving to the next. "Execute at 100% for the thing you're validating, half-ass the rest." This prevents scope creep and gives clean signal.

**7. Re-validate PMF every 3 months for any AI product.**
Elena Verna's mandate. If a cohort that joined 3 months ago is retaining at a lower rate than the cohort that joined 6 months ago — and you haven't changed the product — the competitive landscape has shifted under you. Quarterly: talk to 5 recent signups, 5 churned users. Are they experiencing the same magic the first cohort did?

**8. Ship → distribute → iterate loop velocity is the actual moat.**
Bryan Kim / BAD Guide thesis: features get commoditized when the model layer shifts. The only durable edge is the speed of the loop. For Skale/Lancelot: the advantage is not the current feature set; it's the velocity at which you discover what the next thing is and get it in front of real users. Ship a small version of the next feature to 50 real users this week, not in two months when it's polished.

**9. The right acquisition target for X-native products: the person who posts about the problem publicly right now.**
The people who'd use Skale are posting on X right now about their frustrations with lead gen, cold outreach, audience building. That's your first 100 users. No ads needed. Find 30 of them, DM each one personally with a specific reference to something they posted, offer access.

**10. Build the aha moment into a shareable artifact.**
Bolt.new: the live-build clip. Midjourney: the Discord gallery image. ElevenLabs: the meme voice clip. What is the equivalent for your product — the thing a user naturally screenshots and posts because it makes them look clever/impressive/first? Design for that artifact from the first day.
