## How to Sell (Tyler Bosmeny, Clever / YC) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xZi4kTJG-LE — transcribed — MUST

**Thesis:** Technical founders are already better early salespeople than any rep they could hire — because they have passion and domain expertise. Both trump sales experience.

**Key frameworks:**
- **The Sales Funnel (3 stages):** Prospecting → Conversations → Closing → Revenue
- **Technology Adoption Curve (Everett Rogers):** Only 2.5% of companies are "Innovators" — the only ones who will buy from an unproven startup. You have to reach out to ~100 companies to find 2–3 prospects. Sales is a numbers game first.
- **$100M Sales Motion Map (Christof Janz):** $100K ACV × 1,000 customers (elephants) vs $10K × 10,000 (deer) vs $100/yr × 1M (rabbits). Your price determines your motion — high-touch enterprise sales is economically impossible for $100/yr products.

**Tactics — Prospecting:**
- Network first (warm intro is best). Conferences second (most underrated). Cold email third.
- Conferences: Get the attendee list weeks in advance, email every prospect before arriving. Book your entire schedule in 30-min increments. When Clever went to industry conferences this way, "every single minute of that trip was well spent" and it's where "all of our earliest customers" came from.
- Cold email template that works: short (3-5 lines), personalized, specific why-relevant-to-you hook, ends with direct ask for a call. Never long, salesy, or generic.

**Tactics — Conversations:**
- **The #1 rule:** Shut up and listen. Top 1% salespeople talk 30% of the time and listen 70%. Founders instinctively do the opposite — they talk 70%, kill the deal.
- Ask: "Why did you agree to take my call? How do you solve this today? What would your ideal solution look like?"
- Bosmeny used UberConference's talk-time tracker to discipline his team: if you talked more than the prospect, you probably lost the deal.

**Tactics — Closing:**
- Enterprise redlining: Use YC open-source templates. Don't quibble — "for your first customers, they're manna from heaven." Getting them took 2 months and dozens of emails for Clever's second deal (a $100K/yr customer). This is what success looks like.
- **Trap #1 — Feature requests:** "I'd buy if you built X" → 9 times out of 10 this is a pass. Counter: "We'll build it if you sign now" or "We'll build it when 4 more customers ask for it."
- **Trap #2 — Free trials:** Never. Push back: "We do annual agreements. If you're not satisfied in the first 30 days, you can opt out, no penalty." This gets you commitment AND protects them.
- **Follow-up cadence:** Most deals close after 5+ touches. Almost no founder follows up past the second. Weekly email is appropriate; being persistent is not rude if your emails are thoughtful.

**Numbers:**
- Clever reached out to 400+ companies in their first 2 months at YC
- Closed second deal (100K/yr) after many follow-up emails to someone who was interested but just busy
- 2.5% of companies in your market will even take your call

**Quote:** "You will never discover product-market fit from the nos. You only discover it from the yeses."

**Why in the guide:** Canonical founder-sales primer. Funnel, follow-up discipline, closing traps, and the conference tactic are all directly actionable. No fluff.

## Enterprise Sales (Pete Koomen, Optimizely / YC) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0fKYVl12VTA — transcribed — MUST

**Thesis:** Sales is a learnable skill. The founder is the only person capable of selling before PMF — it requires vision, credibility, and a tight feedback loop to the product. You can't delegate it.

**Key frameworks:**
- **Sales Hypothesis:** "Customer X has problem Y and our product will solve it." A clear hypothesis makes every prospecting decision easy. Optimizely's hypothesis: "Marketers at SMB tech/media/e-commerce want AB tests but can't because tools require code."
- **Demo as movie script:** Open with a recap of the character (your user) and their problem — proves you listened. Walk through a story, not a feature tour. Include "magic moments." Personalize with their logo, their customers, their team's names.
- **Pricing as experiment:** Don't publish enterprise pricing. Quote one price, observe reaction, iterate. Koomen's co-founder quoted $10,000/month, prospect talked him to $2,000 — they still bought. "Initial quote was 5x what they paid and they still bought."
- **Closing is not a conversation, it's a process:** Big companies have security reviews, legal reviews, compliance sign-off. Ask upfront: "How do you buy software? Who needs to sign off?" Then project-manage it alongside your champion.

**Tactics:**
- Anti-pattern: Talking to people who are easy to talk to, not people who need your product. YC founders sell to startups because startups will take calls — but if your product solves a problem companies only have when they get big, this generates useless feedback.
- Top-down vs bottom-up misread: If you need a CIO to sign off, talking to individual contributors wastes months.
- First call goal: NOT to sell. Goal is to qualify (does this person have the problem and budget?) and schedule a demo.
- Demo tip: Optimizely built a feature to run demos on the customer's own website rather than a dummy site. "I knew it was worth it when I saw marketer's eyes light up."
- Implementation: Treat customer implementation as a high-priority internal project. Optimizely closed six-figure deals, then a year later the customer hadn't run a single AB test — the marketing team couldn't get engineering to install it. Lesson: sell a solution, not a product. Implementation is YOUR responsibility.

**Qualifying questions for first call:**
- "What made you decide to take this call?"
- "Tell me about this problem — how long have you had it? How bad is it?"
- "How do you quantify the impact? Why haven't you solved it already?"
- "What's your budget? How does your org buy software? Who makes the buying decision? Who else will weigh in?"

**Quote:** "Sales isn't about tricking people. It's fundamentally about helping people solve their problems, and Engineers are great at doing that."

**Why in the guide:** Best-structured enterprise sales funnel overview for a technical founder. Demo advice, qualification questions, and implementation lesson are all high-yield.

## How to Get Your First Customers (Gustaf Alstromer, YC) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hyYCn_kAngI — transcribed — STRONG

**Thesis:** Startups don't take off by themselves. Founders make them take off. You have to manually recruit customers — it's not enough to push a button on an advertising network.

**Key frameworks:**
- **Startup Curve (Trevor Blackwell):** Launch → launch energy fades → Trough of Sorrow → Wiggles of False Hope → Promised Land. Most companies die in the Trough because founders don't move fast enough with testing.
- **Sales funnel in plain language:** Make the list → Send email → Schedule demo → Talk pricing → Close → Onboard (onboarding often forgotten — it drives retention).
- **Working backwards from a goal:** To get 2 signed customers, map every funnel stage's drop-off. Example: 500 emails → 50% open → 5% respond → 20 prospects → 50% convert to demo → 10 demos → 20% close → 2 customers. Most founders send 100 emails and conclude "sales doesn't work." They didn't have data, just a sample size too small to draw conclusions.

**Tactics:**
- Email format: Max 6-8 sentences. Plain text only (no HTML). Clear language, no jargon. Address their specific problem. State you're the founder. Include social proof (YC batch, impressive prior employers). Include website link with screenshots, not graphics. Call to action at the end.
- Brex example: Enrique and Pedro recruited first 10 customers directly from WW17 YC batch. Email said "10 spots for beta users" (scarcity), described the value prop clearly, noted the specific pain (no personal guarantee required).
- Prioritize easiest customers first: sell to people you know, sell to startups (short decision lines, decision maker often on the first call), sell to the ones most likely to move quickly.
- Raising prices until customers complain but still pay is the right calibration.
- Don't offer free trials in B2B. Offer money-back guarantee or opt-out from annual contract instead.

**Numbers:**
- Example outbound funnel: 500 emails, 50% open, 5% response, 10 demos, 2 customers
- Brex first physical card was just a virtual card — product wasn't finished, but they shipped anyway

**Quote:** "If someone is dragging you along through two or three calls you can always say: 'It's been great getting to know you, but we should talk again in six months.' That's totally fine to say."

**Why in the guide:** Gustaf is the clearest on the math: you need to work backward from your goal, track your funnel, and send more outbound than you think. "Sales doesn't work" is almost always a sample-size problem.

## GTM Boot Camp: Field Sales (Mark Cranney, a16z) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gU_-g00lhm0 — transcribed — STRONG

**Thesis:** Enterprise field sales requires answering three questions in the buyer's mind in sequence: (1) Why should I do anything different? (2) Why you over the competition? (3) Why now? Your sales process is a tool to help the buyer through those three questions — not to communicate your value at them.

**Key frameworks:**
- **Three-question buyer journey:** Needs + cost dominate early (why do anything). Competitive choices dominate middle (why you). Risk spikes late (why now). Your activities and tools should map to whichever question the buyer is currently on.
- **Discovery → Value Framework → Unique Value Proposition:** Don't lead with your solution. First build a discovery tool that maps their initiatives to your capabilities. Then build an as-is / to-be narrative. Only then can you create a truly unique value prop.
- **Organizational layers of decision criteria:** C-level cares about business strategy and ROI. Mid-management cares about architecture/scalability. Users care about features. You need messaging for all three.
- **Champion vs. Coach vs. Supporter (Miller Heiman):**
  - Champion: Sells for you when the door closes. Has influence AND desire to advocate.
  - Coach: Tells you how to navigate internally. Won't actively fight for you.
  - Supporter: Might vote for you but won't give you an edge.
  - You need champions — identify them early, cultivate them throughout.
- **Competitive strategies:** Direct (you clearly win), Divide (get a beachhead, coexist), Develop (delay the decision until you're stronger). Many startups only have Direct as a strategy. Knowing when to use Develop — even trying to stop a deal from happening before you're ready — is often more valuable.
- **Technical Validation Event (TVE):** Define, document, and project-manage the POC/pilot. Get agreement on success criteria upfront. Build the ROI diagnostic simultaneously with the TVE — don't wait until the end to do the business case.

**Tactics:**
- If you get an RFP you didn't help write, you've probably wasted your time. Influence criteria before the formal process starts.
- Build a "seed requirements document" and insert it early in accounts — help prospects define what they should be evaluating.
- "The great general establishes a position where he cannot be defeated and misses no opportunity to exploit weakness of the enemy. The winning general creates the conditions of victory before beginning the war." — Sun Tzu via Cranney
- Process trumps heroics every time.

**Why in the guide:** Best enterprise field sales mechanics playbook available. The three-buyer-questions frame, TVE process, and champion definition are all directly applicable to any complex deal.

## Startup Pricing 101 (Kevin Hale, YC) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jwXlo9gy_k4 — transcribed — OPTIONAL

**Thesis:** Pricing optimization gives you the highest bang for buck of any growth lever, yet it's the most neglected. A 1% improvement in pricing yields a 12.7% improvement in profit — more than acquisition (3.3%) or retention (6.7%).

**Key frameworks:**
- **Pricing Thermometer:** Cost → Price → Value. Margin = price minus cost (incentive to sell). Incentive to buy = value minus price. Value-based pricing maximizes both.
- **Four pricing mistakes:** Pricing too low; underestimating costs; not understanding customer value perception; targeting wrong customers (mainstream instead of early adopters).
- **Sales motion / price alignment matrix:**
  - Under $2K: Self-serve only, inbound marketing, no sales team — conversions same day
  - $2K–$10K: Inside sales, SDR, product demos, 1–3 month cycle
  - Over $25K: Enterprise — phone support, dedicated CSM, sales managers, sales engineers, 6–12 month cycle
  - The "danger zone" (SMB): Complex sales cycle, low ACV — unit economics never work. Escape by either raising price or cutting sales process cost.

**Tactics:**
- The 10x rule: Price should be approximately 1/10 of customer-perceived value. If you charge $100 and it's not obviously worth $1,000 to them, conversion will be hard.
- The 5/20 rule: Raise prices by 5% increments until you lose 20% of deals. That's your optimal price point.
- Early adopters are NOT price sensitive. They care about competitive advantage over all else. Charging too little actually signals reputation risk to them.
- Price optimization is just a table: different price points × conversion rate × revenue. Run the experiment, read the winner.

**Quote:** "If you've never touched the pricing of your product, you're losing out on lots of potential growth."

**Why in the guide:** Practical framework for setting price from first principles. The danger-zone matrix alone saves founders months of mistaken go-to-market effort.

## Easy Sales Concepts So Many Get Wrong (Sam Blond, ex-CRO Brex) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r4MfMumdHsA — transcribed — STRONG

**Thesis:** Nine practitioner concepts most founders execute backwards. The two highest-leverage: don't outsource recruiting (best candidates don't use recruiters), and fix the demand problem before hiring more sales reps.

**Nine concepts:**

1. **Don't outsource recruiting.** Best candidates come from personal networks. Brex's first two reps came via CFO referral; subsequent hires via personal network → their network. External recruiters produce an inferior candidate pool.

2. **Focus on demand until you have too much.** Most founders misdiagnose a demand problem as a pipeline conversion problem. Hiring more AEs into a demand-poor environment just spreads limited demand across more (less-tenured, lower-conversion) reps. Fix: diagnose whether reps are generating 1–2 new opportunities/week. If not — demand problem.

3. **Do things that stand out.** Brex sent champagne bottles to founders as top-of-funnel outreach. Salesforce staged a fake protest outside Siebel's annual conference in 2000. The test: "What are the 2–3 most creative demand-gen campaigns you ran this quarter?" If you can't answer — not creative enough.

4. **Leverage happy customers.** Raving fans post on social, refer customers, speak at conferences — often without being asked. Go above and beyond: if a top customer posts about you and loves Manchester United, send an autographed jersey with a handwritten note. Nobody does this, and it creates magic.

5. **Distribution is as important as the announcement.** On Brex's product launch day (~20 employees), they emailed every influential person each employee knew, asked them all to post simultaneously. "If you were on social that day and follow any tech people, you were seeing a message about Brex."

6. **Be prescriptive on how to buy your product.** Don't let prospects wonder what the next step is. After the demo, describe the "happy path to onboarding": 2-week trial → pricing conversation → limited slots available → here's list price, here's your price. Tell them how to buy.

7. **The presumptive close.** At Brex (free product), end of every demo call: "We have 5 minutes left, let's get you on Brex — here's the application." This increased conversion 2–3x. For paid products: start the trial signup on the same call.

8. **Meet in person.** Post-2020, most sellers default to video. Brex measured: in-person meetings had 3x the close rate of remote. For the right deal size, fly there.

9. **Specialize in implementation/onboarding, not just CS.** Brex found that all LTV, churn, and NPS outcomes tied back to what happened in the customer's first 30 days. Build milestone-based onboarding: define the 3–5 things that make customers sticky (integrate ERP, issue employee cards, migrate subscriptions). Customers stay in "onboarding" until milestones complete, not until 30 days are up.

**Quote:** "Of the nine slides, the first two [recruiting and demand] are the highest-order priority. If you get those right, almost nothing else matters."

**Why in the guide:** Practitioner-level ops detail rarely shared at this granularity. The demand diagnosis framework and implementation milestone model are immediately deployable.

## Building for the Enterprise (Aaron Levie, Box) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tFVDjrvQJdw — transcribed — STRONG

**Thesis:** Enterprise software is a $3.7T market vs $170B consumer. The old rules (slow sales cycle, on-premise, top-down IT procurement) have changed — now you can start with the user, get viral enterprise adoption, and sell top-down after you have usage.

**Key frameworks:**
- **Start intentionally small (the wedge):** Find the sliver of a use case where you can build an extraordinary UX, then expand. ZenPayroll (Gusto) started with just payroll for small businesses, ignoring everything else ADP did. Zenefits found a way to be paid by insurance companies instead of the startup — making their product free to the end customer. Find the thing incumbents can't or won't do economically or technically.
- **User-led vs IT-led paradigm:** Mobile devices flipped enterprise software. IT-led = incumbents win (they own the CIO relationship). User-led = startups win (employees adopt the tool they like, then IT buys it company-wide for security/scale).
- **Find the outlier customers:** Your first customers should be the ones "living in the future" — companies at the bleeding edge of their industry who use technology for competitive advantage. They'll co-design your product in ways generic feedback never will.
- **Build asymmetries:** Do things that are (a) technically infeasible for incumbents (platform-agnostic vs. integrated suite), or (b) economically infeasible (different monetization model).

**Tactics:**
- Listen to customers, but don't build exactly what they say. "Your job is to distill those requests down into the ultimate product." Palantir takes very complex government problems and distills them into simple solutions customers wouldn't have known how to ask for.
- Modularize, don't customize. Build a platform with APIs; let partners/customers build vertical experiences on top.
- Your product should sell itself — but that doesn't mean you don't need salespeople. You need "consultative, domain-oriented sales individuals" who help customers navigate the product AND the competitive landscape.
- Box was in 99% of Fortune 500 within 8 years by entering through the end user, then selling enterprise-wide for security/compliance.

**Quote:** "Consumer DNA at the center of the product means adoption is easier, the product has a higher chance of going viral, and it's easier to sell into the organization."

**Why in the guide:** The user-led vs IT-led framework is the single most important structural insight for enterprise go-to-market today. The wedge + find-the-outlier-customer tactic is the playbook for any vertical-focused startup.

## Sales and Marketing; How to Talk to Investors (Tyler Bosmeny + YC Partners) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SHAh6WKBgiE — transcribed — STRONG

**Thesis (Sales half):** Sales is a core founder responsibility, not a function to delegate. The prospecting → conversations → closing funnel works. Most founders fail on follow-up and close.

**Key additional content (investor pitching half):**

**30-second pitch (3 sentences):**
1. What does your company do? (One sentence. Mom test: can your mom understand it?)
2. How big is your market? (Multi-billion dollar reference is the floor investors want to hear)
3. How much traction do you have? (Specific numbers: MoM growth, revenue, users — or if pre-launch, velocity: "started in January, launched beta in March, product in April")

**2-minute pitch additions:**
4. Unique insight (2 sentences): "Here's something you don't know / the market is wrong about." This is the aha moment — you can see it on the investor's face.
5. Business model (1 sentence): Don't equivocate. Pick the one model that's most accurate and state it. Hedging signals you don't know.
6. Team (2 sentences): Have you made investors money before? No? → How many founders, how many are technical, how long have you known each other, all full-time?
7. The ask: Know your instrument (SAFE vs. convertible note, cap, amount, minimum check size). Use the jargon here — not knowing it signals you're not ready.

**Fundraising tactics:**
- Raise breadth-first, not sequentially. Schedule all meetings in the same 1–2 week window. Hack: "We're building like crazy for the next 2 weeks — can we schedule for week 3?" This groups everyone, signals you're not desperate, and creates social proof.
- One team member raises full-time — it's too distracting otherwise.
- Anything other than a check/wire is a no. "Keep talking to partners" = no.
- Create deal heat — competitive interest drives price and closes.

**Quote (Dalton on fundraising vs building):** "A lot of smart people equate fundraising success with company success. Fundraising is not the goal. Build your company."

**Why in the guide:** The 30-second / 2-minute pitch structure is the canonical framework for pitching. More importantly the "raising in parallel in a single compressed window" tactic is the most misunderstood mechanic founders get wrong.

## The Ultimate Guide to Founder-Led Sales (Jen Abel, Jellyfish) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=969dwgu98qc — transcribed — MUST

**Thesis:** Founder-led sales is the highest competitive advantage a startup has. The founder is the product when there's no brand equity or reference-ability. Your day-one market vision is never the vision that takes you to PMF — the "budding moments" in these calls are where the gold lives. Only a founder can see them.

**Key frameworks:**
- **Sales for research vs. sales for revenue:** Founder-led sales phase is about learning as fast as humanly possible, not closing. "You earn the right to sell." Target: ~$500K–$1M ARR before transitioning. Velocity matters — if you hit $500K really fast, transition. If it took 2 years, stay in it longer.
- **Cold outreach formula:**
  1. Relevancy first (why are you reaching out to THIS person in THIS role right now?)
  2. Something counterintuitive / unexpected (make them say "what?" or "that makes no sense — but wait")
  3. Focus on the problem, not the solution
  4. Keep it short enough to read without scrolling on mobile (3–4 sentences max)
  - Jellyfish's first 20 customers were acquired via cold email. Opening line: "0-to-1 sales talent doesn't exist — that's why I want to talk to you." That's the counterintuitive hook.
- **Qualification signals (is there real pain here?):**
  - Is the problem growing and widening?
  - Are they measuring and managing it today? (No = probably not a priority)
  - How have they tried to solve it? (Hiring, tools, open gap) — the maturity of prior attempts tells you how badly they want a solution.
  - When they start pulling in colleagues to the next call without being asked → you have something.
- **Co-authoring the scope of work:** Turn the prospect into a guide by asking them to co-author the solution design. Reveals their buyer maturity. If they don't have a process to solve the problem yet, they can't buy a technology yet — they need a service first.
- **Services as a step:** 40–50% of B2B companies (in top-down Enterprise sales) need to sell some form of service BEFORE they can sell the technology. The market doesn't yet have a buying process for your category. Go in, get paid to educate, design their process, earn the logo, then earn the technology contract. Time-box service engagements to 90-day increments.
- **Procurement mechanics:**
  - Know who the signatory is before entering procurement.
  - Do the work FOR procurement: fill out their forms, make their job easy.
  - Be classified clearly — if they can't place you, you'll get the "kitchen sink MSA" (insurance requirements, liability clauses, $5M bonding). Avoid by being specific about what you do and don't do.
  - Truncate contracts: separate service contract (gets you in fast) + technology contract (follows after service proves value).
  - Enterprise deals close in 6–12 months typical; regulated industries 9–12 months. Once you're in, a $100K deal becomes $500K next year, $1M the year after.

**Abel's biggest finding:** "Everyone says they have a bottom-of-funnel problem. I've never actually seen a bottom-of-funnel problem. It's always a top-of-funnel problem — qualification. Spending time on the wrong leads equates to zero."

**Numbers:**
- Conversion rate from outbound to booked call: 2–3% at floor, 5–7% mature, 8–15% if founder-led + sharp PMF
- Win rate (calls to closed deals): 15–30% is healthy; win rate determines how much volume you need in the funnel
- Enterprise ACV target for early-stage: $50K–$200K; sweet spot $50–100K
- Initial sales cycle: 90 days (exceptional), 6–12 months (typical Enterprise)

**Quote:** "Trust is the number one currency in sales. If you are a trusted founder, your market will continually send you leads and word-of-mouth. If you're just trying to get a logo on your deck, they'll churn in six months and you'll have destroyed trust."

**Why in the guide:** Most comprehensive tactical founder-sales guide in the corpus. The procurement mechanics section alone is worth the listen — nobody else covers this at this depth.

## Inside Clay's Sales Playbook to $100M ARR (Becca Lindquist, Clay) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d_nWswprCqk — transcribed — STRONG

**Thesis:** Clay scaled to $100M ARR with ~200% NDR by hiring non-traditional sales talent, introducing variable comp late (early Stripe-style salary-only is "f***ing stupid"), and building a winning-culture attainment distribution (60% over 100%, 80% over 80%).

**Key frameworks:**
- **Champion definition (3 requirements):** 
  1. Sells for you when you're not in the room
  2. Has access and influence over the economic buyer
  3. Has a personal win tied to your product succeeding (career advancement, building their brand, becoming "the AI person")
  Focus on personal win first — the rest follows.
- **PLG + sales motion:** With usage-led products, your job isn't to land logos — it's to "suck the oxygen out of the account." Once you have a foothold, expand to all other teams and workloads before competitors get in. Think of it as securing territory, not closing transactions.
- **Hire two reps at a time:** "You hire one, you don't know if it's good or bad. You hire two, it's pretty clear which one's better." At Heap, they hired Lindquist and Todd expecting to fire one — kept both because both were excellent.
- **Comp design:** 
  - Early-stage (first 2 reps at Heap): Artificially low base (~$60K), 25% commission on every deal closed, 33% on 2-year deals. Net of paying back base monthly. This aligned incentives perfectly.
  - Scale (Clay today): Competitive OTE with structured accelerators. Quota-to-OTE ratio: 7.5x (6–10x is the AI-era norm; below 4x is weak).
  - Culture benchmark: 60% of reps over 100% quota, 80% over 80% = winning culture where people help each other and recruit friends.
- **Hiring signals:**
  - Green flag: Can they think critically about a customer's specific business and tie your product to a real dollar outcome? Not "here's what the product does" but "here's how this would impact JPMC's forecast accuracy."
  - Green flag: College athletes — learned to work hard without supervision; easier to teach to work smart than vice versa.
  - Red flag: Jumpy career (under 2 years/company). Red flag: 12+ years at one company (stopped learning, "settled").
  - Green flag: Data-centric resume (% increases, specific metrics, not "President's Club").
  - Test for coachability by giving feedback in the interview process and watching how they receive it. Defensiveness is the biggest red flag.

**Quote:** "It's pretty hard to sell something nobody wants. It's pretty easy to sell something everybody wants." / "Look for a company with unwavering product-market fit. Clay: we haven't churned a customer. Our NDR is close to 200%. Those are the types of things you look for."

**Why in the guide:** Clay is the canonical AI-era GTM playbook. PLG-to-enterprise expansion, comp design, and champion mechanics are all battle-tested at $100M ARR.

## Do Things That Don't Scale (Paul Graham) — https://paulgraham.com/ds.html — read — MUST

**Thesis:** The thing that separates successful startups is not the product — it's the founder's willingness to apply extraordinary, unscalable effort in the early days. Growth is compound: 10% weekly from 100 users = 14,000 in a year, 2M in two.

**Key frameworks:**
- **Startup as vector:** Not just a product to build — also a set of unscalable initial tactics that have to be run simultaneously. Founders underestimate this second dimension.
- **Fragility window:** Early-stage startups are exceptionally fragile. Airbnb's survival was determined by approximately 30 days of intensive personal user engagement in New York. Most founders underestimate how close to death they are.

**Five unscalable tactics (every one applies):**

1. **Manual user recruitment ("Collison installation"):** When a user agreed to try Stripe, the founders didn't send a link — they took the laptop and set the account up immediately. "Collison installation" is now YC terminology. Stripe still pursued aggressive user acquisition even though they were solving an urgent problem.

2. **Delight through attentiveness:** Create an "insanely great user *experience*," not just an insanely great product. Early startups can provide service levels no large company can match. Wufoo sent hand-written thank-you notes to every new user. This scales better than you think because it embeds in company culture.

3. **Narrow market / "contained fire":** Facebook restricted to Harvard, then to each college one at a time. This made users feel the product was authentically theirs. B2B: other startups are ideal early customers because they're open to novelty and grow alongside you.

4. **Consulting-like service:** Viaweb built online stores for merchants manually. "You're being compensated as a product company — extra attentive — not as hourly consultants expected to solve everything." Do what it takes to make each early customer insanely successful.

5. **Hybrid manual-to-automated:** Stripe's "instant" merchant accounts were manually set up behind the scenes. Launch faster, build muscle memory for future automation.

**Anti-patterns:**
- Big launches rarely matter. Successful startups almost never have memorable launches.
- Partnerships with large companies almost never generate early growth. "More work than expected, minimal returns."
- Any growth strategy that omits extraordinary initial effort is "ipso facto suspect."

**Permanent DNA point:** Unscalable early tactics shouldn't be abandoned at scale — they reshape permanent company culture. Aggressive early acquisition becomes embedded as an operational principle.

**Quote:** "The most valuable insights you can have about startups are insights into which of the things you're worried about don't matter. The most common mistake early-stage founders make is working on things that don't matter because they seem like the right startup thing to do."

**Why in the guide:** Primary-source argument for doing founder-led sales one customer at a time. The five tactics and the fragility-window concept are the most important mental model resets in early-stage GTM.

## $100M Offers — Free Offer Creation Course (Alex Hormozi) — https://www.acquisition.com/training/offers — partial — MUST

*(Full course content is behind login / not directly fetchable. The below is synthesized from the BAD_GUIDE's own summary of this framework + widely-cited public material.)*

**Thesis:** Stack so much value that the price becomes a rounding error relative to the outcome. Don't compete on price — compete on the gap between what you charge and what it's worth.

**Key frameworks:**
- **The Value Equation:**
  > Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) / (Time Delay × Effort and Sacrifice)
  
  The four levers:
  - Dream Outcome: What does the customer want to achieve? (for Skale: qualified pipeline booked)
  - Perceived Likelihood: How confident are they it will work? (case studies, guarantees, social proof increase this)
  - Time Delay: How fast do they get the result? (faster = more valuable)
  - Effort and Sacrifice: How hard do they have to work? (less friction = more valuable)

- **The Grand Slam Offer:** A combination of product + bonuses + guarantees + scarcity/urgency that makes the price look absurdly cheap relative to the value stack. Hormozi's example: gym offer went from "personal training $500/month" to "6-week body transformation program — or your money back — includes 3 training sessions/week + nutrition guide + daily accountability check-ins + group community access" at $1,500/month. Same product, 3x price, higher conversion.

- **Charge for the outcome, not the deliverable:** Don't price per seat, per API call, or per hour. Price against the dollar outcome the customer achieves. Palantir's rule: if you save a customer $2M, charge $500K. The customer who accepts outcome measurement is serious.

**Tactics:**
- Find the one bottleneck that, if removed, makes everything else work. Build your offer around removing THAT bottleneck.
- Guarantees shift risk to the seller — this is what allows premium pricing. Structure guarantees around outcomes, not refund windows.
- Scarcity and urgency are legitimate when real (limited onboarding slots, beta cohorts). Never fake them.

**Quote (guide's framing):** "For Skale, the dream outcome is qualified pipeline. Cut the time-to-first-meeting and the perceived-likelihood (via case studies and guarantees) and you can charge 3x."

**Why in the guide:** Forces a reframe from "what do I charge for what I do" to "what is the outcome worth and how do I make delivery feel obvious at any price point."

## Finding Language/Market Fit (Matt Lerner, First Round) — https://review.firstround.com/finding-language-market-fit-how-to-make-customers-feel-like-youve-read-their-minds/ — read — STRONG

**Thesis:** Most "product isn't ready" or "market isn't ready" diagnoses are actually "message doesn't land." When your copy sounds like the sentence already in the customer's head, conversion jumps from 0.5–3% to 8–40%. Language-market fit is the fastest, cheapest thing to find — and it should be Step Zero, before you touch the product.

**Key frameworks:**
- **Neuroscience of attention:** Buyers operate in goal-directed mode (searching for something specific) not stimulus-driven mode. They pattern-match your headline against their mental target in nanoseconds. Your job is to look like "food" to their foraging mind.
- **Jobs-to-Be-Done:** Customers don't buy products — they hire them to accomplish specific jobs in their life context. "The words in their heads will reflect their situation, not your product."
- **The 4-step process:**
  1. **Customer interviews:** Find someone who recently purchased your product or competitor alternative. Walk through the purchase journey from beginning. "When did you first realize you were struggling? What alternatives did you try? Why that purchase on that specific day?" Capture verbatim language — not your interpretation.
  2. **Draft headlines from their language:** Complete "Now you can ___" (not "Our product is ___"). Use their words, not yours. Avoid: "all-in-one," "faster," "revolutionary," "convenient." Use specific tasks and outcomes.
  3. **5-second test (qualitative):** Show headline in large text for exactly 5 seconds. Ask three people: Can you recall it? What would this do for you? What does it mean in your own words? Iterate until comprehension is instant.
  4. **A/B test in paid ads (quantitative):** Test on Facebook/Instagram — faster than landing page tests.

**Conversion examples:**
- Weak: "Accelerate your learning. Develop your thinking through online discussion" → **3% conversion**
- Strong: "Organize, share and take notes on web pages, PDFs and academic papers easily, so you can learn faster, remember more and get things done" → **30% conversion**
- Lerner moved a product from **0.5% to 40% conversion** on copy changes alone.

**Winning headline patterns:**
- Kevel: "Build a custom ad server in just weeks"
- Storemaven: "Increase app store conversion rates and pay less for every install"
- Popsa: "Create Photobooks in 5 minutes"
- Calm: "Find your Calm. Sleep more. Stress less. Live better."

**Tactics:**
- Early Nike and iPhone ads led with functional/technical specifics — not "Just Do It" or "Think Different." Aspirational messaging requires decades of awareness first.
- Google search "people also searched for" and keyword tools (SEMRush, Ubersuggest) to validate terminology choices.
- "Investing app" has 2x the search volume of "stock trading app" — the word you choose signals your understanding of the customer.
- Avoid: "better than X" — the moment you say it, the buyer asks you to define it, then decides to give the incumbent another year.

**Quote:** "If you do not have language-market fit, put down your tools and go find it. Until you find language-market fit, nothing else will really work."

**Why in the guide:** Conversion is usually a language problem, not a product problem. The 5-second test is deployable in an afternoon. This is one of the highest-ROI activities in early GTM.

## How to Win Your First 10 B2B Customers (Lenny Rachitsky) — https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-win-your-first-10-b2b-customers — read — STRONG

**Thesis:** All first-customer acquisition operates as concentric circles of trust. High-trust channels (personal network, investor network) convert first. Low-trust channels (cold outbound, press) come later. No single channel works for everyone — but almost every successful company used exactly one channel well.

**7-channel sequence (roughly in order of trust → ease of conversion):**
1. Personal network (friends, colleagues, prior coworkers)
2. Strategic cold outbound (targeted, creative, personalized)
3. Investor networks (YC batch, First Round portfolio, angel intros)
4. Community participation (forums, meetups, events where ICPs congregate)
5. Content and following (Twitter, blogs, newsletters)
6. Press
7. Product launch (HN, Product Hunt)

**Case studies by channel:**

**Personal network:**
- Figma: Dylan Field recruited design-oriented founder friends
- Gong: "All of the first dozen customers were some sort of personal connection"
- Notion: Started with founder friends in SF startup ecosystem

**Strategic cold outbound:**
- Retool: Used Crunchbase to filter by recent fundraising + operationally-heavy verticals (delivery, fintech, not SaaS). Emailed CTOs and VP Ops. Deliberately avoided SaaS companies (don't build internal tools).
- Zip (procurement startup): Exclusively LinkedIn outreach asking for ADVICE, not a demo. "We just want to understand the problem." Very advice-oriented vs. sales-oriented.
- Figma: Built a script to identify influential Twitter designers, cold-DM'd them.

**Investor / YC networks:**
- Vanta: CEO emailed 10+ years of Bookface posts mentioning compliance. "Got our first 10 customers through YC." Targeted old YC companies with compliance concerns — NOT current batch-mates (too speculative).
- Amplitude: Focused on ex-Zynga PMs who'd started companies — shared experience created warm trust.

**Community:**
- Snyk: "Promoted across open-source maintainers and dev communities." Attended meetups.
- Databricks: Organized AMP Camps — 70-person hackathons — to introduce potential customers to the software directly.

**Key meta-patterns:**
- Trust is the primary currency. "It's all a matter of trust. Somebody I trust says use this tool — I'll trust it way more than if a marketing team says it."
- **PR rarely converts.** Almost every founder says press is the least effective channel despite trying it. Slack's 8,000 beta requests day one were impressive but most came from product-led viral, not press.
- **YC batch-mates don't work.** Target established YC alumni (have budget, real problems), not current batch (too speculative, not representative customers).
- **Friends aren't liability** — they give you faster, more honest feedback. Use them.
- Canva: no silver bullet — "always bits and pieces." Took 2 years to build 10,000-person wait list.

**Quote:** "Half of our first 10 customers came through Sean [co-founder]. Everybody knows Sean." — Census

**Why in the guide:** Best synthesized first-customer playbook from real companies. The channel-by-type-of-product mapping and the trust-concentric-circles model are the most useful mental models for prioritizing early acquisition effort.

## Fastest Growing B2B: First Ten Customers (Lenny Rachitsky) — https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-todays-fastest-growing-b2b-businesses — read — STRONG

**Thesis:** The fastest-growing B2B companies used one of three primary tactics: (1) personal network, (2) where customers congregate, or (3) press/launch. Personalization, repetition, and "buy the relationship before the product" dominate.

**Core tactics from landmark companies:**
- **Slack:** "Begged and cajoled friends" at other companies to be beta users
- **Stripe:** Collison installation — set up accounts on the spot during demo, friends invited their friends
- **Shopify:** Built the Ruby on Rails developer community (DHH, Tobi — organic endorsements from thought leaders)
- **Plaid:** Dev forums, IRC, meetups, accelerator networks
- **Square:** Door-to-door local merchants, visited "multiple times per week" for feedback
- **Workday:** "Customers bought the friendship, not the software." Early enterprise sales = relationship trust before product trust
- **Intercom:** Founder sent hand-crafted emails showing each recipient a customized version of the product in their own context
- **New Relic:** Earned endorsements from Rails thought leaders (DHH, Tobi) — community respect required to earn, not just buy

**Notable numbers:**
- Slack: 8,000 beta requests day one, 15,000 by week two
- Shopify: "few hundred stores" from initial Rails waitlist
- Canva: 50,000 wait-list signups from pre-launch press

**Meta-patterns:**
- "Collison installation" principle: Remove the friction between interest and usage. Don't send a link. Walk them through setup.
- Tastemaker effects: Get 3–5 influential community members using your product and everyone else follows.
- Word-of-mouth amplification: When you deeply involve early customers in product development, they become evangelists.

**Why in the guide:** Historical grounding for the seven-channel framework. The Workday "buy the friendship" and Intercom hand-crafted-email examples are the most memorable and instructive first-customer stories in the corpus.

## The SaaS Org Chart (David Sacks, Craft Ventures) — https://sacks.substack.com/p/the-saas-org-chart — read — STRONG

**Thesis:** SaaS organizations follow a predictable hiring sequence from Seed through Series C. The most common and fatal mistake: hiring a VP Sales before you have a repeatable close. The second: misunderstanding when each function earns its headcount.

**Hiring roadmap:**

**Seed → Series A ($1M ARR, ~50 employees):**
- Sales: VP Sales leads; 2 Enterprise AE + 2 Mid-Market AE + 2 SMB AE + 2–4 SDRs + 1 Sales Ops
- CS: Director leads; 2 Enterprise CSM + 2 Mid-Market CSM + implementation/solutions + support
- Marketing: Director leads; demand gen + product marketing + sales enablement + events
- Key ratio: 1 SDR : 2 AE

**Series A → B ($5M ARR, ~125 employees):**
- CRO appointed; sales org grows to ~45 people
- SDR team grows to 6; Sales Ops expands to 3
- Marketing CMO appointed, 15 people, product marketing expands to 4

**Series B → C ($20M ARR, ~400 employees):**
- Geographic segmentation: US + EMEA as separate orgs
- SDR team grows to 25 (with 2 managers)
- Each segment (Enterprise, Mid-Market, SMB) gets dedicated leadership
- Sales training function added (5 in US, 3 in CS)
- Revenue per employee: $50K–$67K ARR/employee at Series C

**Revenue per employee benchmarks:**
- Series A: $20K–$25K ARR/employee
- Series B: $40K–$50K ARR/employee
- Series C: $50K–$67K ARR/employee
- IPO average: ~$100K ARR/employee

**Critical sequencing rules:**
1. Don't hire a VP Sales until you personally have a repeatable close.
2. Don't hire Sales Ops until you have at least 10 AEs — before that the complexity doesn't justify it.
3. Marketing and CS headcount should scale alongside Sales, not lag it.
4. Pattern: AEs before SDRs, Sales leadership before Sales Ops.

**Why in the guide:** The clearest map of what the org should look like at each stage. Most useful as a "do not hire this yet" checklist — stops founders from over-hiring sales leadership before the motion is repeatable.

## Section Body — The Guide's Own Wisdom for This Section

### ▸ You can't hire a sales team to find a market you haven't found yourself
Founder-led sales is not a phase you suffer through until you can afford a VP Sales. It is how you find the repeatable motion that a rep can later run, and there is no other way to get it. Hiring sales before you have 10 customers and a script that closes is how seed companies burn 18 months. David Sacks' org chart exists to stop exactly this mistake.

### ▸ Do-things-that-don't-scale applies to sales hardest of all
Hand-sell the first 10–50 customers one at a time. The manual, unscalable work is where the signal lives. Every awkward call teaches you the vocabulary, the objection, the real buyer. You have no excuse not to use the outreach tools you build on yourself first.

### ▸ Make the price irrelevant with a value stack, don't discount
Hormozi's Grand Slam Offer: stack so much value that the price becomes a rounding error against the outcome, instead of competing on being cheap. His Value Equation — (dream outcome × perceived likelihood) / (time delay × effort) — tells you which lever to pull. The dream outcome is qualified pipeline; cut the time-to-first-meeting and perceived-likelihood (case studies, guarantees) and you can charge 3x.

### ▸ Charge for the outcome, not the seat
Palantir's rule: if you save a customer $2M, charge $500K, not $10K for "API access." Outcome pricing forces conviction on both sides and makes the relationship sticky. Price against the result delivered, not the input consumed. The customer who accepts outcome measurement is the serious one.

### ▸ Your conversion problem is usually a language problem, not a product problem
Matt Lerner's language/market fit: customers convert when your copy sounds like the sentence already in their head. Most "the product isn't ready" diagnoses are actually "the message doesn't land." Test the words before you build features. Lerner moved conversion from 0.5% to 40% on copy alone.

### ▸ AI SDRs only amplify a motion that already works
AI SDRs only work when sales already works. AI scales whatever motion you feed it. If your ICP is wrong or your script doesn't close, you've just automated the broken version at volume. Don't automate outreach until you've closed by hand and know the script converts.

### ▸ Follow-up is the mechanic everyone skips
Bosmeny's data: most deals close after the fifth-plus touch, and almost no founder follows up past the second. "Always be closing" is wrong; persistent, structured follow-up is the actual skill. Build the cadence as a system, not a vibe.

**The guide's framing:**
> "The 90% treat sales as a function to delegate and a funnel to optimize. The 1% treat early sales as product research that happens to collect money. You are not closing deals; you are extracting the exact words customers use about their pain, the trigger that made them look, and the budget line the spend comes out of."

## → Top Actionable Takeaways for @Seventydotleven

**1. Spend the first 30–60 days doing founder-led sales personally, every day.**
One founder owns it full-time (Clever model). Track your funnel rigorously: outreach → response → demo → close → conversion rates at each stage. You need 300–500 outreach attempts before your data means anything. Don't conclude "sales doesn't work" from 50 emails.

**2. Cold outreach formula: relevancy + counterintuitive hook + problem focus + fits on mobile.**
Do NOT lead with what you built. Lead with why this is relevant to THIS person's specific role right now, then one counterintuitive claim that makes them say "wait, what?" Never pitch the solution in the first message. Max 3–4 sentences. Test in Gmail's audio preview to catch passive-aggressive tone.

**3. Get to language/market fit before touching the product roadmap.**
Run the 5-second test on your headline. Ask 5 recent customers to walk you through their purchase journey verbatim. Steal their exact words. Your current copy is probably your words, not theirs. A 10–30x improvement in conversion is available from language alone.

**4. Price against the outcome, not the tool.**
What is the dollar value of a qualified meeting for a B2B founder? If a booked meeting is worth $5K–$50K in pipeline, charge based on meetings generated — not seats or API calls. Test the Value Equation: Dream outcome × perceived likelihood = the number you anchor to; price should be 1/10 of that.

**5. Build a value stack, not a pricing page.**
Grand Slam Offer: product + onboarding service + guarantee + limited cohort access. Make the price look cheap relative to the stack. Include a risk-reversal guarantee (outcome-based or money-back). Scarcity should be real (limited onboarding slots work because your time IS constrained early).

**6. The conference tactic is massively underused.**
Find the 2–3 annual conferences where your exact ICP gathers. Get the attendee list weeks in advance. Email every relevant prospect before you arrive. Book every 30-minute slot. One well-executed conference day can produce more qualified pipeline than 6 months of cold email.

**7. Never offer a free trial. Offer an opt-out clause on an annual agreement instead.**
"We do annual agreements. If you're not satisfied in the first 30 days, you can opt out with no penalty." This gets you commitment, revenue, and validation — while protecting the buyer's risk perception. Free trials give you none of those.

**8. Nail the 30-second pitch / 2-minute pitch structure.**
30 seconds: what you do (mom test) + market size + traction. 2 minutes: add unique insight + business model (one sentence, no hedging) + team + the ask (with the jargon). Practice until these feel like conversation, not recitation. The more you talk, the more you risk saying something that loses them.

**9. Don't hire a salesperson until you have a repeatable close you can document.**
The Sacks org chart's most important rule: you hire a VP Sales AFTER you've personally closed 10+ customers and can articulate exactly what the ICP is, what objections arise, and what script closes them. Hire before that → you waste 18 months and $300K.

**10. Champion mechanics: the three requirements.**
In any multi-stakeholder enterprise deal, identify who (a) sells for you when you're not in the room, (b) has influence over the economic buyer, and (c) has a personal win tied to your product winning. Focus on surfacing the personal win first — that's what turns a coach into a champion. "What would it mean for YOUR career if this worked?"

**11. First 30 days of implementation are the entire LTV.**
Brex tied all customer lifetime value, churn, and NPS back to first-30-day milestones. Define 3–5 "sticky actions" for your product. Build a milestone-based onboarding checklist. Customers stay in implementation until milestones complete — not until a fixed time window passes. Re-onboarding customers who missed milestones is nearly impossible.

**12. Distribution matters as much as the announcement.**
Before any product launch, funding announcement, or press piece: build the spreadsheet of every influential person each team member knows. Coordinate to post simultaneously on launch day. The event itself is 50% of the value; the distribution network you activate for it is the other 50%.
