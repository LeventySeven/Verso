# Primitives-not-wrappers + AI moats (positioning)

## Why AI Moats Still Matter (And How They've Changed) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fgzr3PhzIMk — transcribed

**Speakers:** David (VC), Alex (VC/founder, TrialPay background), Martine — a16z / Foundation Capital style panel on moats in the AI era.

### Differentiation vs. Defensibility — the central distinction

- AI is an incredible tool for **differentiation** (e.g., voice agent speaks 50 languages, 24/7, fully compliantly) but the AI capability itself is NOT a source of defensibility — it is "so consensus, like cloud was not consensus, mobile was not consensus."
- **Defensibility** of a software product resides in: owning the end-to-end workflow, the context in which it's applied, becoming the system of record, having a network effect, deeply embedding within the customer. These heuristics have not changed.
- The moat is the context layer and the workflow, not the model capability.

### What's fundamentally different this cycle: software IS the labor

- "The software itself can actually do the work" — market opportunity is no longer just IT spend, it's labor.
- You used to not be able to hire someone for $1 to do a task. Now you can hire software for $1. Taxi analogy: post-Uber, people took far more rides because the cost and friction collapsed. Same dynamic for AI agents across every domain.
- This means software gets MORE deeply embedded in customers, not less, because replacing AI labor is harder than replacing SaaS licenses: "Is it more difficult to replace that software with another piece of software or to rehire that team?"

### Moats: still the same five

1. **Workflow ownership / system of record** — owning the end-to-end workflow is the primary moat. The messy-inbox wedge strategy: hook into unstructured data sources (email, fax, phone), extract structured data, plug into downstream system of record — then expand laterally. Example: Tenor (healthcare inbox → scheduling → prior auth → eventually the system of record).
2. **Network effects** — still present; data network effects are scale-dependent ("gravity" — you need to reach mega-scale before the moat is evident; at 4 customers vs 3 customers there's no difference; at 4 billion vs 1 billion customers, the difference is clear and compounding).
3. **Deep embedding / switching costs** — when software does the labor, switching means replicating organizational knowledge, not just migrating data. Far higher switching cost than traditional SaaS.
4. **Scale effects** — not network effects but economies of scale (Amazon: delivery speed becomes possible because so many people buy, enabling distributed warehouse network). "Get to gravitational scale" where you can build the factory.
5. **Goldilocks zone of irrelevance** — the janitorial services problem: if you tell a CEO you can get their toilets 9% cleaner and save 1% on janitorial spend, they don't even exercise the mental energy to find who cares. Hard to get in; also very hard to get out. Examples: ADP, Paychex. These companies are "stuck for good." Strategy implication: don't try to sell to them; wait for greenfield (new company creation).

### Feature → Product → Company ladder

- A **feature** is a marginal improvement on an existing product (Chrome plugin; voice receptionist sitting on top of existing scheduling software).
- A **product** is a system of record or meaningful workflow owner (own browser).
- A **company** has a sustainable 10–20 year moat path (own browser company that makes money).
- **What's changed:** in the AI era, features can reach revenue scale faster than ever — "$20,000/year for the front office receptionist of an orthodontic clinic" because you're replacing LABOR, not IT spend. But the feature must backfill product, then backfill company, as quickly as possible before the platform it rides on competes.
- The feature is the wedge, not the moat. Drew Houston (Dropbox) knew it was a feature; he had a plan to backfill it into a $10B company before Apple could catch up. That plan is the requirement.
- "I've just never seen a world where features can get to revenue scale as quickly" — fastest path to initial customers because buyers are solving labor problems, not evaluating software categories.

### GPT wrapper risk

- 18 months ago "GPT wrapper" was a pejorative. "If the model capability and the application capability are very overlapping, you're in a risky spot."
- But: the Cambrian explosion of interesting markets means OpenAI is NOT going to build orthodontic clinic answering software. "If they've done that, I would be short OpenAI because it means they've run out of good stuff to do."
- Plural model providers (5+ serious contenders + open source) removes the Windows risk of a single platform owner capturing everything.
- The application value often comes from orchestrating work across multiple model companies — which a foundation model provider vertically integrating would actually limit.
- Dan Rose (Facebook business development) story: "You're pitching me a gold brick 100 feet away. We have gold bricks at our feet." Big model companies will pursue the horizontal big enterprise plays, not the Goldilocks-zone niches.

### Platform tax vs. platform competition

- Always dangerous to build on somebody else's platform: they can change the tax at will (10% → 40%).
- Two questions: (1) Will the platform owner compete with me? (2) If yes, am I in the Goldilocks zone? VisiCalc → Lotus → Excel: Excel won because the spreadsheet was intrinsically linked to why people bought computers — NOT Goldilocks zone, direct competition.
- Dropbox survived because: hard to do well technically, and in the Goldilocks zone (Apple should have done it but never will prioritize it because it doesn't triple their profits). Platform owners get lazy on features that don't move their core P&L.

### Market consolidation dynamics

- "Jack Welch: you have to be number one or number two; there's no value in number three through 100." This hasn't changed.
- 20 competitors all loss-leading with VC money → bad market → bottom 15 go bankrupt → consolidation → good business. The process happens the same way in AI.
- Model companies are "the most cutthroat" category: "unless you're state-of-the-art and you're trying to earn a living, that's just not going to work."
- Specialization may be the optimistic escape valve: creative tools, up-market video production, etc. — specialization can create defensible niches if market is growing fast enough.

### "Context is king" — the David thesis

- "Founders today are often younger and more technical, less often native to the industry, but fluent in the toolset."
- Still need to hire for domain context very early. Example: Eve (legal AI, plaintiff law) — founders from Rubric (infrastructure), hired plaintiff attorneys on staff so every model release gets evaluated against real workflow impact.
- The sources of defensibility reside in "figuring out ways to apply that technology in the context of your specific customer."
- Best alignment: AI reinforces the business model rather than competing with it. Eve operates on contingency → no billable-hour erosion → unlimited AI adoption incentive → 5x efficiency = 5x clients.

### Incumbents vs. startups in AI era

- AI is consensus (unlike cloud, unlike mobile) — no equivalent of Steve Ballmer saying "nobody will buy an $800 phone with no keyboard." Every incumbent is trying to embrace it, adding "a button or a feature."
- Incumbents probably win defensively in Goldilocks zones (Tata/Infosys: add AI to existing JP Morgan integration, drop headcount, keep the contract).
- Green field requires: (a) patient entrepreneur who won't try to sell to locked-in incumbents, and (b) high rate of new company creation. EHR example: near-zero new hospital systems created → impossible to displace Epic/Cerner with green field strategy.
- Per-seat pricing → first to be rationalized during downturns (1,000 Salesforce licenses for a 200-person company). Outcome-linked pricing (payroll, usage-based) far stickier.

## Generative AI's Act Two (Sequoia) — https://sequoiacap.com/article/generative-ai-act-two/ — read

### Act 1 vs Act 2 Framework

- **Act 1 (Technology-out):** Discovery of the "hammer" (foundation models), novelty apps, lightweight exploration. Fastest-ever adoption: ChatGPT to 100M MAU in 6 weeks (Instagram: 2.5 years; WhatsApp: 3.5 years).
- **Act 2 (Customer-back):** Solve problems end-to-end. Foundation models are one piece of a comprehensive solution, not the entire solution. Custom editing interfaces, stickier workflows, better outputs, multi-modal.
- **The transition signal:** Stop asking "what can this model do?" and start asking "what does this customer need end-to-end?"

### The Value Problem (Act 2's core challenge)

- "Generative AI's biggest problem is not finding use cases or demand or distribution, it is **proving value**."
- The $200B question: "What are you going to use all this infrastructure to do?"
- **Retention crisis:** Median DAU/MAU for GenAI apps = 14% vs. 60-65% best consumer companies, 85% WhatsApp. Only exception: companionship (Character AI, 2-hour average session).
- Path: fix retention → prove daily value → earn the right to expand workflow → become the system of record.

### Where data moats actually are

- "Data moats are on shaky ground" for application-generated data — next-gen foundation models may obliterate any data moat a startup generates.
- **Better moats: workflows and user networks.** These are more durable than proprietary training data.
- Counter-intuition: the moat isn't in the model, it's in the workflow and the network of people using the product together.

### Agentic systems as the Act 2 product form

- Moving from autocomplete/human-review to autonomous problem-solving ("from level 0 to level 5 autonomy").
- External tool access + end-to-end problem solving = the product shape.
- **System-wide optimization:** Not individual workflow efficiency but holistic system optimization (e.g., autonomously resolving support tickets or PRs to improve the entire system).
- Chain-of-thought, tree-of-thought, reflexion as reasoning techniques improving complex reasoning.
- LangChain enabling multi-chain invocation and debugging — Act 2 is about orchestration, not prompting.

### Vertical integration reality

- Most successful user-facing applications have been vertically integrated (model + application layer together).
- Customers setting up vendor-agnostic infrastructure between model providers (anticipating commoditization at model layer).
- Predicted horizontal separation between application and model layers hasn't cleanly materialized.

### RAG and infrastructure primitives

- RAG: bringing business/user context to reduce hallucinations, increase truthfulness. Vector databases (Pinecone) as backbone.
- RLHF and fine-tuning becoming more accessible — domain adaptation at lower cost.
- LLMOps (Langsmith, W&B) for evaluation, improvement, production monitoring — Act 2 requires operational discipline.

### What the killer apps have in common (Act 1 evidence)

- Midjourney: hundreds of millions in revenue, 11-person team. Creative muse as primitive.
- Character AI: 2-hour average session. Entertainment/companionship as primitive.
- GitHub Copilot: 55% more efficient. Developer workflow embedding.
- Harvey: custom LLMs for elite law firms — vertical workflow ownership.
- Glean: workspace crawling/indexing for enterprise relevance — the data layer primitive.

## Aggregation Theory (Stratechery) — https://stratechery.com/2015/aggregation-theory/ — read

### The Three-Part Value Chain

- Every consumer market: **Suppliers → Distributors → Consumers/Users**
- Pre-internet: distributors integrated BACKWARD into supplier relationships because suppliers were scarce and distribution was expensive. "In a world where transactions are costly, owning the supplier relationship provides significantly more leverage."
- Newspapers: controlled printing + distribution + content. Hotels: brand trust + property inventory. Taxis: dispatch + medallions + fleet.

### The Two Internet Inversions

1. **Free distribution of digital goods** → distributor advantage neutralized. Suppliers no longer captive.
2. **Zero transaction costs** → viable for a distributor to integrate FORWARD with end users at scale.
- Result: competition plane inverts. Best UX wins users → users attract suppliers → suppliers improve UX (virtuous cycle). "Suppliers can be commoditized, leaving consumers/users as first-order priority."
- **Aggregators often don't pay suppliers.** They aggregate modularized suppliers to gain consumer relationships.

### What each aggregator modularized

| Aggregator | What incumbents integrated (backward) | What aggregator modularized |
|---|---|---|
| Google | Publications bundled articles + distribution | Individual pages — directly accessible |
| Facebook | Publishers bundled content + ad placement | Direct advertiser targeting without publisher proxy |
| Amazon | Publishers controlled editing + distribution | Distribution as modular service; then e-books (unlimited access) |
| Netflix | Networks bundled broadcast + content | Entire library on-demand, any time |
| Uber | Dispatch + fleet management | Fleet management (independent drivers) |
| Airbnb | Hotels bundled rooms + brand trust | Trust (reputation system replaces brand) |

### Winner-Take-All Mechanism (precise loop)

1. Aggregator builds superior UX → attracts disproportionate user share
2. Large user base attracts suppliers (more supply = better product)
3. Better supply improves UX → compounds advantage
4. "All of the examples... are not only capable of serving all consumers/users, but they also **become better services the more consumers/users they serve** — and they are all capable of serving every consumer/user on earth."

### Three Historical Waves

1. **Wave 1 (digital-native):** Google — modularized content, monetized via advertising proxy.
2. **Wave 2 (user-generated):** Facebook, Snapchat — users ARE the suppliers; exclusive content by design. "Even stronger position than Google" because of supply exclusivity.
3. **Wave 3 (physical industries):** Airbnb, Uber — digitized non-obvious functions (trust, dispatch). Suppliers not exclusive in theory, but increasingly in reality because of the virtuous cycle.

### Framework for Identifying Future Aggregators

Three diagnostic questions:
1. What is the incumbent's critical differentiator?
2. Can that differentiator be digitized/commodified?
3. If yes, does the new entrant win on UX and trigger the virtuous cycle?

"Nearly every industry will belatedly discover it has a critical function that can be digitized and commodified."

### Conservation of Attractive Profits (Christensen connection)

- Profits shift when one company modularizes the incumbent and integrates another part of the value chain.
- Incumbents (backward-integrated) lose value. Aggregators (forward-integrated with users) gain it.
- The profit doesn't disappear — it moves. Predictable direction: toward whoever controls the consumer relationship.

### What makes a moat durable vs. temporary

- **Durable:** Exclusive consumer relationship at scale + virtuous cycle + global scalability + UX superiority embedded in incentives.
- **Vulnerable:** If a new competitor provides meaningfully better UX (competition always remains on UX plane); if users multi-home; if suppliers re-integrate forward.
- "The profound changes caused by the Internet are only just beginning; aggregation theory is the means."

## Good News: AI Will Eat Application Software (a16z) — https://a16z.com/good-news-ai-will-eat-application-software/ — read

### Core Thesis

- AI won't destroy software; it will expand it, but create **software bifurcation**: high-value delivery companies win; thin-value/outdated models lose.
- "Code is never where the value has lived." Software companies sell: encoded organizational workflows, switching costs, network effects, proprietary data + institutional knowledge, process engineering, brand/trust.
- Better models make the application layer MORE capable, not thinner. "Hard part was never raw intelligence. It was knowing what to do with it."

### Seven Moats Under AI (Hamilton Helmer Framework)

| Moat | AI Impact |
|---|---|
| Switching costs | Weakens (agents reduce migration friction) — but forces better products |
| Network effects | Strengthens (collaboration platforms compound) — Salesforce ecosystem, Figma, Harvey |
| Scale | Conditional (token economics + regulatory consolidation) — Stripe, Shopify |
| Brand | Strengthens ("no one got fired for buying IBM") — especially for business-critical functions |
| Cornered resources | CRITICAL + amplified — Bloomberg market data, Abridge clinical conversations, VLex legal DB become MORE valuable |
| Process power | Becomes DOMINANT — workflow orchestration deepens faster than replication capacity |
| Counterpositioning | New entrant advantage — Decagon per-conversation pricing vs. Zendesk per-seat (incumbent can't cannibalize own revenue) |

### Process Engineering as Dominant Moat

- George Sivulka (Hebbia): "workflow orchestration becomes harder to replicate as complexity deepens."
- Harvey: firm-specific templates, review processes, partner preferences → switching cost once embedded.
- Implication: the application layer is NOT a thin wrapper — it IS the product. Models are an input, not the differentiator.

### AI-Native Disruption Mechanics

- **Thin wrapper vulnerability:** Frontend tools as "commodity functionality" face pressure. "Hostages, not customers" → switching-cost logic inverts for pure wrappers.
- **Counterpositioning example:** Decagon charges per-conversation/per-resolution. Zendesk charges per-seat. Zendesk cannot adopt outcome-based pricing without cannibalizing its own installed revenue base (Blockbuster/Netflix parallel; PeopleSoft/Workday parallel).
- "System of record to system of action" — the transition that creates new winners.

### Market Dynamics

- Software ETFs down 30% since 2026 start; Salesforce, Adobe, Intuit, ServiceNow, Veeva down 25-30%. Fear: "SaaSpocalypse."
- Rebuttal: "world is still short software" — demand expansion outpaces margin compression.
- Per-seat → per-outcome/per-conversation pricing enabled by agents. Value alignment improves.
- Agents optimize for price, bypassing brand loyalty — but "reports to humans who have to worry about getting fired." Brand solves the infinite-choice problem for risk-averse buyers.

### What survives vs. dies

- **Dies:** Archaic interfaces + annual price hikes; thin wrappers; outdated monetization; systems of record without workflow innovation.
- **Thrives:** Genuine value delivery; process-embedded companies; network effects owners; proprietary data leverage; trusted brands in business-critical functions.
- Platform shifts historically produce winners LARGER than predecessors: Netflix > Blockbuster TAM. AI application winners will be larger than prior SaaS winners.

## → Verso Implications

### 1. Verso must be a primitive, not a workflow orchestrator

- The Sequoia Act 2 frame and the a16z thesis converge: value accrues to whoever owns the **workflow** and the **user context**, not to whoever provides the model or the generic orchestration graph. LangGraph, CrewAI, and LangChain are all in the Sequoia Act 1 / "GPT wrapper" zone — they provide graph machinery without owning a workflow primitive.
- Verso's primitive is the **agent loop** itself — the ReAct + MCP + memory + evals loop that every agent must be built on. This is the right layer to own: low enough to be structural (not a workflow), high enough to encode real engineering leverage (not just an HTTP client).
- Analogy from Aggregation Theory: Verso should modularize the "agentic orchestration" function that today is tightly integrated into every framework-specific graph DSL (LangGraph's StateGraph, CrewAI's crew config). Modularize it → expose clean MCP-first tool interface → own the developer relationship.

### 2. The moat Verso must build is not the loop — it is the ecosystem that accretes to the loop

- From the AI Moats talk: moats only become evident at mega-scale (the gravity/anti-fraud analogy). Verso should not over-index on "our ReAct loop is better" — that is differentiation, not defensibility.
- The defensible moat path: tool registry (MCP server ecosystem) + eval corpus + memory backend → each of these creates a data network effect that becomes harder to replicate at scale. The more agents and tools are registered against Verso's primitives, the better Verso gets at routing, eval scoring, and memory retrieval. This is the moat.
- From a16z: cornered resources (proprietary data) and process power (workflow depth) are the top two moats under AI. Verso should architect for accreting both: eval traces as proprietary data, tool integrations as process depth.

### 3. Verso must avoid the feature trap — build toward product, then platform

- The AI Moats talk's Feature → Product → Company ladder applies directly: Verso starts as a "cleaner agent loop" (a feature relative to LangGraph). It must backfill to a product (the full agent runtime: loop + tools + memory + evals as a coherent deployable unit) and then to a platform (the place developers build agent applications on top of, with Verso as the taxing layer).
- The "messy inbox wedge" pattern: start with the single most painful wedge (agent loop simplicity vs. LangGraph complexity), expand upstream and downstream from that wedge, eventually become the system of record for agent behavior.
- Risk: if Verso is still "just a loop" when a model provider (OpenAI, Anthropic) decides to ship a first-party agent runtime, Verso is in VisiCalc territory. Must reach platform depth before that happens.

### 4. Verso's positioning should exploit counterpositioning, not head-on competition

- From a16z: Decagon's per-conversation pricing is structurally uncopyable by Zendesk because Zendesk can't cannibalize its per-seat base. Verso's equivalent: **MCP-first, graph-optional** design is structurally uncopyable by LangGraph (which has bet its entire API surface on graphs) without a complete rewrite.
- Verso should actively name this: "We don't force graphs. If you need a graph, use the escape hatch. But most agents don't need graphs, and forcing graphs costs you 3x complexity for 0 benefit." This is a counterpositioning claim that incumbents cannot rebut without admitting their default is unnecessarily complex.
- From Aggregation Theory: the winning aggregator modularizes what the incumbent integrated. LangGraph integrates: graph topology + agent state + tool dispatch + memory + checkpointing all into one StateGraph object. Verso modularizes: each concern is a separate, clean primitive. The developer relationship (exclusive DX ownership) goes to whoever provides the best experience.

### 5. Verso should own the developer UX so completely that it becomes the default for new companies (greenfield strategy)

- From the AI Moats talk: greenfield strategy requires patient entrepreneur + high rate of new company creation. Agent frameworks are in a greenfield moment: every new AI startup is spinning up an agent runtime from scratch. Verso doesn't need to displace LangGraph's installed base immediately — it needs to be the default choice for every new agent project.
- "Nobody wants to be locked into a piece of software for 20 years — they want to solve the problem in front of them." Verso's wedge is: solve the agent loop problem so cleanly that every new agent project starts with Verso. The platform emerges from the install base, not the other way around.
- The "paying for a dollar of labor" insight: developers adopting Verso are replacing the cognitive labor of designing agent architecture from scratch. Make that trade so favorable (3 lines to a working ReAct loop vs. 150 lines of LangGraph StateGraph) that the adoption is reflexive.

### 6. Verso must not confuse AI-as-capability with AI-as-moat

- From the AI Moats talk: AI capability gives differentiation (your agents work); workflow ownership gives defensibility (developers can't leave). Verso's MCP-native tool integration, eval harness, and memory backend must be designed to accumulate switching costs over time — not just to be technically excellent.
- Practical design implication: Verso's memory backend should not be a thin adapter to Redis. It should be a first-class memory primitive with its own schema, retrieval semantics, and eval hooks — such that switching to a competitor means re-engineering all of that, not just swapping an import.
- Same logic for evals: eval results stored in Verso's format, versioned against specific tool/loop configurations, become organizational knowledge that's extremely painful to migrate. This is the process power moat from a16z.

### 7. Aggregation Theory: Verso should aggregate the MCP tool ecosystem, not just support it

- The Aggregation Theory frame says: whoever controls the consumer (developer) relationship, with modularized suppliers (MCP tool servers), wins. Verso should be the aggregator of MCP tool servers — the place where tool servers register, developers discover and import tools, and the ecosystem grows.
- This is the Aggregation Theory move: modularize what was previously integrated (bespoke tool implementations per framework), aggregate the tool registry, own the developer relationship. Tools become commoditized; Verso captures the value.
- Warning from the same theory: exclusive supplier relationships are what makes aggregator moats deep. Verso should invest early in an MCP tool server registry where being "Verso-certified" means something — early tool servers get discovery advantage, which creates lock-in on the supply side.

### 8. Verso's escape hatch for durable execution is a product-level decision, not an API footnote

- From Sequoia Act 2: "system-wide optimization" and "holistic problem solving" are where the Act 2 value is. Durable execution (checkpoint-resume, HITL, long-running workflows) is a real Act 2 need.
- But from the AI Moats talk: features must backfill to products. Durable execution is a feature of the Verso product (available when needed) — not the core primitive. The core primitive remains the simple ReAct loop. The escape hatch must be architecturally clean: using it should not contaminate the 80% of agents that don't need it.
- Design rule: the durable execution API should be opt-in at the `agent.run()` call, not a separate class hierarchy (which is the LangGraph mistake — making every agent a subgraph of a StateGraph).
